## The wide-bandgap chip question: where does the money actually sit
Here is the belief we went in to test. In wide-bandgap power electronics, the chips that switch high voltage more efficiently than silicon, the durable value is not in the transistor itself. It has moved off the die and onto three other layers: the system architecture, the intellectual property, and one hard materials chokepoint. This round hardened that picture with fresh evidence, and it also complicated it.

Start with the die. It is commoditising, fast. Silicon-carbide six-inch wafers fell from about fifteen hundred dollars to about five hundred in a year, a two-thirds drop. Nameplate capacity is running near three million wafers a year against real demand closer to two million. Wolfspeed, the silicon-carbide leader, went through Chapter Eleven bankruptcy and came out with its debt cut by about seventy percent. On the gallium-nitride side, the Chinese maker Innoscience sells at roughly half the Western price, and Navitas, the best-known pure-play, saw revenue fall about forty-seven percent this year. Making a better chip, on its own, is not protecting anyone's margin.

So where does the money go? First, to system architecture. Google, Microsoft and Nvidia are standardising an eight-hundred-volt direct-current power system for AI data centres through the Open Compute Project. That is an open standard, a socket that names its winners, not a chip advantage. Second, to intellectual property, which is now a courtroom fight: a Chinese court found Infineon infringed an Innoscience patent and ordered a sales halt, while a US trade body ruled the other way. Third, and most durable, the raw material. China makes over eighty percent of the world's gallium and runs its exports under a licensing regime that expires on the twenty-seventh of November this year. Western buyers pay around three thousand dollars a kilogram against about two hundred and thirty-four inside China. That premium is the one thing a better chip design cannot engineer around.

The complication: the neat split between gallium-nitride for low power and silicon-carbide for electric-vehicle traction is starting to leak, because cheap silicon-carbide is now invading gallium-nitride's own kilowatt sockets. For a Cambridge lens, the honest answer to "where does the UK make money in chips" is not the die and not the substrate. It is integration, IP and epitaxy: Cambridge GaN Devices and its monolithic design, the newly rebranded Semiconductor Catapult, and the compound-semiconductor cluster. The realistic outcome for that kind of company is a trade sale into a larger firm's data-centre power roadmap, not a standalone franchise. Confidence in the refined thesis rises to sixty-six percent; the clean-band version slips to about thirty.

## The Cambridge, London and UK deep-tech map this fortnight
Now the wider map, across three fields: AI for science and materials; quantum, chips and photonics; and climate and energy. The pattern this fortnight is that Cambridge is where the deepest science companies sit, London is where the money and the certification platforms cluster, and the rest of the UK holds a few genuine frontier bets.

In AI for science and materials, the centre of gravity is Cambridge. CuspAI, the AI-materials-discovery company, closed a four-hundred-and-fifty-million-dollar round earlier this summer at around a two-point-six-billion-dollar valuation and launched an AI Materials Foundry with dozens of partners. In London, a Cambridge spin-out called Flower Labs released a frontier-class general AI model built for local, private deployment. The reference names to know here are CuspAI, Isomorphic Labs on the drug-discovery side, and Flower.

In quantum, chips and photonics, Cambridge again leads. Riverlane, which builds the error-correction layer that makes fragile qubits usable, opened a US headquarters in Maryland and signed a cooperation agreement with the neutral-atom company Infleqtion to put its error-correction tools onto their hardware. Alongside Riverlane sit Nu Quantum in quantum networking and Quantinuum in trapped ions. This is also the field of the wide-bandgap chip story, where the UK's real seat is integration and IP rather than the raw device.

In climate and energy, the action is spread out. In Cambridge, three companies built through the Carbon13 venture builder raised money: Ki13 took a five-million-dollar seed to turn waste biomass into green hydrogen at roughly half the electricity input of water electrolysis; a biochar startup raised over half a million pounds to strip forever-chemicals from water; and a weather-forecast benchmarking startup raised nearly three hundred thousand. In London, the carbon-certification platform Isometric extended its Series A to around fifty million dollars, and the Clean Growth Fund, one of the few dedicated UK climate venture firms, reached eighty-one-and-a-half million pounds on the second close of its second fund, anchored by UK pension money. And in Oxford, First Light Fusion demonstrated a key low-cost compression principle on its in-house pulsed-power facility.

Put the three fields together and the connective tissue is compute. AI-materials work needs high-performance computing; quantum needs photonics and control electronics; and the whole thing needs firm, clean power. The UK is genuinely strong in the science and the IP, and thinner where volume manufacturing and large late-stage cheques are concerned.

## The wide lens: the demand engine has changed hands
Step back to the whole field, and the single biggest shift is who is now paying for clean energy and hard technology. It is no longer climate policy or climate-specialist capital. It is the artificial-intelligence compute build-out.

The clearest proof came from geothermal. Fervo Energy brought its Cape Station project in Utah onto the grid, the first time a utility-scale next-generation enhanced-geothermal plant has delivered anywhere in the world. It switched on an initial thirty-three-megawatt unit on a project heading toward five hundred megawatts, and the power is being sold to serve, in the company's own framing, the AI boom. Firm, around-the-clock clean power now has a buyer with a balance sheet: the data centre.

Meanwhile the money that used to fund climate directly is drying up. Climate-tech venture fundraising is heading for its worst year in a decade, on track below one billion dollars for the first time since twenty-fifteen, with only a handful of specialist funds closed and two vehicles accounting for nearly eighty percent of what was raised. The capital did not vanish. It rotated into AI, where a single company is reportedly raising thirty billion dollars at a valuation over a trillion.

In heavy industry, green steel split again. Mesabi Metallics announced a fifteen-billion-dollar lower-carbon steel complex in Iowa, ten million tonnes a year, using direct-reduced iron and an electric-arc furnace instead of coal. But it runs on natural gas, not green hydrogen: lower-carbon, not zero-carbon. That is the pattern across the sector. The gas route gets financed and built; the hydrogen route stays stalled. And in the United States, federal climate policy is in active retreat, with fuel-economy standards just cut.

The through-line for the next couple of years is blunt. Build for the compute buyer's balance sheet, not for the grant. Technologies with a standalone financial case that a hyperscaler or an industrial will buy on cost and performance are winning; the ones that needed a subsidy or a green premium are being left behind.
