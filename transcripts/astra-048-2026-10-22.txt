## Where the money sits in ceramic electrochemistry
Here is the belief this session tested, and where it landed. In high-temperature ceramic electrochemical cells, the kind that run as fuel cells one way and as electrolysers the other, the durable, financeable seat for a British player is not owning the factory. It is owning the intellectual property and licensing it. The reference case is Ceres Power, spun out of Imperial College, which licenses its steel-backed solid-oxide stack to partners in Japan, Korea, China, Taiwan and India and lets them carry the capital cost of building the plants.

Across this session the evidence hardened that view, and confidence moved from sixty-two to about sixty-seven percent. Ceres reported first-half revenue up eight percent to twenty-two point eight million pounds, its operating loss roughly halved, a gross margin above seventy percent, and a hundred and seventy-two million pounds of cash after a fresh raise. First royalties have started to flow, though management is honest that they only scale from twenty twenty-eight. Meanwhile the opposite model, building the electrolyser factory yourself, is visibly retreating. Topsoe is mothballing its five-hundred-megawatt Danish factory, and thyssenkrupp Nucera has exited high-temperature electrolyser series production altogether.

There is one genuine counter-case worth stating plainly, because a thesis that only hears agreement is not being tested. Bloom Energy, a vertically integrated American manufacturer, just posted over a billion dollars of quarterly revenue, growing a hundred and sixty-five percent, and it is profitable. So integration can out-earn licensing at scale. But Bloom sells on-site power, not electrolysers, into a different market. The honest verdict is a confirmed split: the asset-light licensing seat is real and uniquely suited to a science-rich, factory-poor country, and the low-temperature protonic-ceramic frontier that might one day undercut it remains stuck at laboratory scale, centimetres across, with durability measured in hundreds of hours. The thing to watch is not the chemistry. It is whether a scaled integrated manufacturer, not the new chemistry, becomes the real threat to the licensor.

## The UK deep-tech map this week: materials, quantum and energy
Step back from the single thesis and look at the whole British deep-tech map across three sectors, because the landscape is the point. Start with artificial intelligence for science and materials. The defining Cambridge company is CuspAI, which raised four hundred and fifty million dollars in July at a two point six billion dollar valuation and then spent it assembling a foundry of more than forty-five chemical and semiconductor partners, supplying compute and data rather than selling a model. In London, Orbital Industries, founded by a DeepMind alumnus, licensed its materials-discovery platform to the chemicals giant BASF in August. The pattern across the sector is consistent: the generative model itself is being given away, and the value migrates to proprietary data, wet-lab validation, and the industrial partner that actually makes the material.

Now quantum, chips and photonics. The freshest signal is Salience Labs in Oxford, which raised a hundred and twenty-five million dollars this month for optical circuit switches aimed at AI data centres, backed by AMD, Intel Capital and the British Business Bank. In Cambridge, Riverlane sells quantum error-correction, the decoder layer every quantum computer will need, and is targeting a million error-free operations by the end of this year. Quantinuum, straddling Cambridge and Colorado, is valued at ten billion dollars. The tell here is the same as in materials: Britain is strong at invention and early capital, and short on the industrial scale-up layer where the margin finally lodges.

Third, climate and energy. Ceres Power anchors the licensing story. On the capital side, Clean Growth Fund, one of only a couple of dedicated British climate venture firms, held an eighty-one and a half million pound second close in September toward a hundred and fifty million target, which matters because a fund that is actively closing is itself a hiring and dealflow signal.

Pull it together and the three sectors rhyme. AI-for-materials needs high-performance computing, quantum needs photonics, and clean energy needs both. Britain is genuinely strong at the science and the early company, and genuinely weak at the factory. Which is exactly why the licensing seat, the one Ceres occupies, keeps being the part a British investor can actually underwrite.

## The wide lens: the 1.5 degree debate just quietly ended
While the deep dive went narrow, here is what the rest of the field did. The biggest shift this week was framing, not physics. The United Nations Environment Programme's annual emissions gap report concluded that holding warming to one and a half degrees with no meaningful overshoot is, in its words, no longer feasible. Current policies track to about two point eight degrees by the end of the century. The official conversation has moved from avoiding overshoot to managing its size and duration, and that quietly turns durable carbon removal from an optional extra into a load-bearing part of every credible pathway.

Second, a useful corrective on energy prices. New British analysis found that expensive gas, not green levies, is behind nearly ninety percent of the rise in household electricity bills since twenty twenty. The political reflex blames net zero. The data says the opposite: the remaining exposure to gas is the problem, and electrification, heat pumps and clean power, is the hedge. That reframes decarbonisation as the cost-control argument, not the cost.

Third, the AI build-out became a line in the climate ledger rather than a footnote. One new report warns that American data centres could erase decades of air-quality gains, even as carmakers bet that compute and AI are how they compete. Artificial intelligence is now both a demand shock on the grid and the tool being sold to clean it up, and that tension no longer nets out quietly.

The long view across all of this: the centre of gravity is shifting from can we still hit one and a half degrees, now answered, not without overshoot, to who owns the firm power, the carbon removal and the efficient electrons that make overshoot survivable and affordable. That rewards exactly the assets worth tracking: dispatchable power, durable removal, grid capacity, and the materials and efficiency layer that lowers the cost of electrifying heat and heavy industry.
