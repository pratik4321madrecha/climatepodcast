## Why Thermal Batteries Win in Europe and India but Lose to US Gas
Confidence on the thermal-battery thesis moved from seventy-four to seventy-six percent today, and it is now firmly split. Here is what a thermal battery is. You run cheap wind or solar electricity through a big resistance heater, like an enormous toaster, and heat solid blocks of carbon or firebrick to well over a thousand degrees. Then you discharge that heat on demand as industrial steam. Round-trip efficiency is above ninety-five percent. The physics is settled.
So I computed the delivered cost of heat myself. Take a capital cost of thirty dollars per kilowatt-hour of thermal storage, ninety-five percent efficiency, daily cycling, an eight percent discount rate. The fixed cost floor is about three dollars twenty per million British thermal units. Everything on top of that is the price you pay for electricity to charge it. Charge at twenty dollars a megawatt-hour and delivered heat costs about nine dollars forty. That number matters because Electrified Thermal Solutions, one of the makers, publicly claims it beats gas above roughly nine dollars fifty. My bottom-up number landed on their break-even. The model is sound.
Now the split. United States industrial gas is about four dollars twenty per million BTU. Thermal heat cannot beat that unless the power is nearly free. But German industrial gas is around eighteen dollars, and Indian spot liquefied natural gas is around twenty-five. In those markets thermal heat at nine dollars wins by two to nearly three times. Same battery, opposite verdict, decided by geography.
The evidence on the heat side got stronger this month. Holcim is putting two Electrified Thermal batteries into a cement plant, the first named marquee heat customer. Antora and POET built a five gigawatt-hour unit in under twelve months. Rondo's German unit with Covestro runs by the end of this year. The heat business is bankable and real. The power side, converting heat back to electricity with thermophotovoltaics, is still pre-demonstration. Fourth Power only began commissioning its demo on the fourteenth of September, with no efficiency published at all.

## Firm Power and the Queue: What the Rest of the Field Did
The wider field this week kept pointing at one pattern: the binding constraint has moved from generation cost to the grid queue. The United States installed a record twenty point two gigawatt-hours of grid batteries in the second quarter, yet the same week the Energy Information Administration framed interconnection and permitting, not equipment, as the bottleneck for the twenty-four gigawatts planned this year. Grid hardware is where scarcity is concentrating. Germany's 50Hertz awarded a two gigawatt North Sea converter platform, part of a two and a half billion euro order. Firm power kept compounding: the International Atomic Energy Agency raised its nuclear outlook for the sixth straight year, and next-generation geothermal drew a hundred and thirty-five million into Mazama Energy, with ConocoPhillips and Shell's venture arms participating.
The tell of the week was in carbon accounting. Isometric, a London measurement-and-verification certifier, extended its Series A to fifty million with the Intercontinental Exchange joining.
