## deep-dive
The deep dive this week is about who pays for a place in the UK grid queue.

Start with the physics. A one megawatt AI rack fed at fifty-four volts needs about eighteen and a half thousand amps. At eight hundred volts it needs about twelve hundred and fifty. So delivering power, from the substation to the rack, is the limit on AI compute, and a grid connection is the first link.

The queue is large. The national grid operator counted about seventy-three gigawatts across about three hundred and fifteen datacentre projects in September, against a Great Britain peak of about forty-five gigawatts. Holding a place has cost little, so people asked for more than they would build.

Ofgem's consultation proposes to price it. According to a law firm summary, the fee would be between two hundred and thirty-seven thousand five hundred and seven hundred and twelve thousand five hundred pounds per megawatt, for projects above forty megawatts, working as credit security. Milestones would also require a credible end user and long-lead equipment orders. The consultation is closed and Ofgem says a decision is aimed for the end of the year.

Here is my arithmetic. At five hundred megawatts the fee ties up between about a hundred and nineteen and three hundred and fifty-six million pounds. The Government estimates a five hundred megawatt site saves about eighty million pounds a year with the proposed power discount, so the money at risk is one and a half to four and a half years of that saving. That is my multiplication from two secondary summaries, not Ofgem's calculation.

One more point: the discount zones named are Scotland, Cumbria and the North East. None is in East Anglia. That is an inference from those named zones.

Confidence in the inquiry stays at seventy-eight percent, because the decision is still pending. A queue that is free to join is proof of cheap options. A priced one gets requested by people with a customer.
