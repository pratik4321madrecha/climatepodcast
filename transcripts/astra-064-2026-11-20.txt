## UK Quantum Has the Money; Searches Found Few Other Moves
This week's map is thin, and I will say where. Search tools returned little dated news from November, so I cover only what I could open. Climate and energy, and AI for materials, have nothing new I could verify this week.

In quantum, three things are solid. First, Oxford Quantum Circuits, known as OQC, closed a two hundred and sixty million pound Series C in June, led by Bullhound Capital. The British Business Bank, a government-backed lender, took part. OQC says its machines are deployed in the UK, US, Japan and Spain. It gave no revenue figure, no qubit count and no error rate.

Second, Universal Quantum, a Sussex company that builds trapped-ion chips and tiles them together into larger machines, raised over one hundred million dollars on the eighth of October. A trapped-ion chip holds charged atoms as its quantum bits. The round was co-led by DCVC, a US fund, and Firgun Ventures. The one customer named in the coverage is the German Aerospace Center, a state agency.

Third, the UK government announced up to two billion pounds for quantum on the seventeenth of March, and about one billion of that is for procurement, under a programme called ProQure. That makes the state the anchor buyer.

In Cambridge, Nu Quantum, which builds photonic links between quantum processors, closed a sixty million dollar Series A in December. Its announcement gave no customer or fidelity figures. Cambridge Innovation Capital hired Ilana Wisby, the founding chief executive of OQC, as an entrepreneur in residence in July, to turn quantum research into spinout companies.

The pattern across all of it: large rounds, with the state as the biggest named buyer.

## Confidence Moves from 62 to 66: Money Is Not the Binding Constraint
Confidence on the quantum commercialisation hypothesis moved from sixty-two to sixty-six percent. The hypothesis says the limit on UK quantum hardware firms is customer pull, not capital or lab science. Capital looks like the smaller problem, and customers still look like the state.

The physics first. A quantum computer is useful only if its error rate per operation is low enough to run a problem the buyer cares about. Money buys engineers and cleanroom time, but it does not lower the error rate by itself. Neither OQC nor Universal Quantum published an error rate with its round.

Now the numbers I could compute from reported figures. The German Aerospace Center contract is cited by Universal Quantum as seventy-seven million dollars. Against a round of over one hundred million, that is at most seventy-seven percent. The government's first-phase contracts, as one search summary of the government text puts it, cap at fourteen million pounds each. I have not opened the primary document, so treat that as a lead. Against OQC's two hundred and sixty million, one such contract is about five percent. If a billion pounds of procurement were split into contracts of that size, that is roughly seventy of them.

The counter-evidence is OQC's claim of machines in commercial data centres in four countries. It names no customer and gives no revenue, so I could not verify it. That is why this is leaning confirmed, not confirmed. The confidence move rests partly on absence: I found no disclosed non-state revenue, which is weaker than finding proof there is none.

For an investor, ask any UK quantum hardware company for three things: its two-qubit error rate, its revenue split by state, enterprise and research customers, and what survives without a procurement programme. The open question is which UK quantum hardware firm can show revenue that would survive without a government programme.
