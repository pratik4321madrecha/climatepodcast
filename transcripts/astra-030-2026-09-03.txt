## Why the hot brick is the easy part
Confidence on the thermal-battery thesis moved from sixty to seventy-one percent today, and the verdict is confirmed with one sharp correction. Here is the mechanism. You take cheap, intermittent renewable electricity and run it through a resistor to heat a big block of cheap material. Firebrick in Rondo's case, to fifteen hundred degrees Celsius. Solid carbon in Antora's case, to twenty-four hundred degrees. Electrified Thermal made the firebrick itself the heating element, so the brick and the resistor are the same object. You store the heat, then blow air through it or open a shutter and let it radiate out as process heat on demand.
The reason this matters is cost. The storage medium is between ten and fifty times cheaper per kilowatt-hour than a lithium-ion battery. Antora's carbon costs about one dollar per kilowatt-hour of stored heat. Firebrick systems, in peer-reviewed work, land around eleven dollars. Lithium-ion is over a hundred and thirty. And refractory brick already cycles inside industrial furnaces for decades, so the durability question is largely answered before you start. Heat round-trip efficiency is above ninety-five percent. The device is not the risk.
So what is the risk. Two things, both outside the box. The first is the price of natural gas. At four-dollar American gas, independent modelling from Berkeley says your electricity has to cost under about fourteen dollars a megawatt-hour for the heat battery to win on cost alone. That is below almost any sustained wholesale price. So in cheap-gas Texas, the hot brick loses, not on physics, on arithmetic. In Europe, where delivered gas is three to four times more expensive, the math flips and it wins today. The second risk is getting the cheap electrons to the box at all. The American interconnection queue is about two and a half terawatts, with waits around four and a half years. That is why the winning move is behind-the-meter siting, plugging into the customer's existing connection and selling heat as a service, which is exactly what Antora's five-hundred-fifty-million-dollar round is funding.
One more correction, and it is the contrarian point. The headline number everyone repeats is that thermophotovoltaic cells convert heat back to electricity at over forty percent. That is true for a single cell in a lab. The best published module is around eleven percent, and nobody has independently shown a megawatt-scale array. So these are excellent heat batteries and, for now, unproven power batteries. Underwrite the heat. Treat the electricity story as a twenty-twenty-eight option, not a fact.

## The five hundred and fifty million dollar vote for heat, not power
Antora's Series C is a vote on the heat business specifically. Five hundred and fifty million dollars, co-led by G2 Venture Partners and Eclipse, bringing the company to about seven hundred and seventy million raised. What did they actually build. A five gigawatt-hour thermal battery at a POET ethanol plant in South Dakota, two hundred modules, commissioned in under twelve months, charged on wind, displacing coal boilers under a long-term heat contract. Rondo is running a hundred megawatt-hour unit at a California oil field at over ninety-seven percent round-trip. Electrified Thermal turned on its first commercial twenty megawatt-hour system in January.
Now put that against the rest of the storage market, because the contrast is the story. Form Energy, the iron-air long-duration leader, raised seven hundred and fifty million this month, but at a down round, its valuation cut from three billion to under two. Base Power, doing backyard batteries, raised a billion at thirteen billion, up from four. Cash is available. Valuations are not. And the market is paying up for distribution and duration, not for cells. Thermal batteries sit on the good side of that trade, because they compete on duration and on siting, which is where the value is migrating.

## Tata Steel is the unsubsidised proof
The cleanest proof that the financial-first case is real comes from India, not America. Kraftblock, a German company, installed a twenty megawatt-hour heat battery at Tata Steel's plant in Jamshedpur. It captures waste heat from the sintering process, cuts about twenty-two thousand tonnes of carbon a year, and, critically, it was built with no subsidy. The chief executive said it is performing better than they calculated. That is the whole thesis in one sentence. India is a live, unsubsidised market for industrial thermal storage because delivered energy is expensive and heavy industry is concentrated. For anyone thinking about a second market after the UK, this is where the unsubsidised deals already close.

## The transition is becoming demand-led
Step back to the whole field, and one pattern connects almost everything that moved this fortnight. The transition is turning from supply-led to demand-led, and the demand is data centers. Fervo signed the largest enhanced-geothermal deal ever with Google, three hundred and ninety-six megawatts with an option to a gigawatt. TerraPower is naming a data-center customer for a reactor whose molten-salt storage lets it follow AI load. Grid-flexibility software Emerald AI raised a hundred and fifty million at a billion-dollar valuation to throttle data centers to the grid. Fusion crossed seven billion in cumulative private funding.
The second pattern is that capital is bifurcating and geography is shifting. US policy is in retreat, paying RWE over a billion dollars to abandon offshore wind leases and setting solar tariffs that lift module prices almost forty percent. Green hydrogen keeps hitting write-downs, Plug Power walked away from its Antwerp plant. But the storage tax credit survived, and money is rotating toward Europe, India, and emerging markets where the unsubsidised numbers already work. The long view: over the next two to three years the scarce inputs are firm power, a fast grid connection, and cheap clean electrons, not working devices. Whoever shortens the path from an electron to a delivered unit of heat wins.
