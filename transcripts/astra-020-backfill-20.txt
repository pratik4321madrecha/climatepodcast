Cement is the world's most-used material and its single largest industrial CO2 source because of one step: making clinker. Clinker is the grey nodule you get by cooking limestone and clay in a kiln at about 1,450°C. Two thirds of cement's carbon comes from that step, and only a third of it is the fuel. The rest is chemistry you cannot switch off: heating limestone (calcium carbonate) drives off CO2 to leave the calcium oxide that makes cement set. Grind clinker with a little gypsum and you get ordinary Portland cement (OPC). Cement is roughly 8% of global emissions, and the process CO2 is why you cannot simply electrify your way out of it.
There are only three physical ways to cut that carbon, and they are not equally hard. One, invent a new binder that sets without a clinker kiln. Two, delete the kiln by making clinker inside a furnace that already exists, for example the electric-arc furnace (EAF) that recycles scrap steel. Three, keep OPC but substitute 30 to 60 percent of the clinker with a supplementary cementitious material, an SCM: a reactive powder that behaves like cement in the mix but was never fired in a clinker kiln. Historically the SCMs were industrial wastes, fly ash from coal plants and GGBS (ground granulated blast-furnace slag) from steel. The frontier version is a manufactured SCM, a pozzolan made on purpose from abundant rock or clay.
Define it once
SCM: a powder that reacts like cement but skips the clinker kiln. LC3: limestone calcined clay cement, clinker part-replaced by heated kaolin clay plus limestone. Take-or-pay: a buyer commits to pay for volume whether or not they lift it. Book-and-claim: the buyer pays for the carbon attribute while physical product goes elsewhere, so no molecule actually moves to them.
The question was which route becomes a financial-first buy rather than a green-premium one, and after three sessions the answer is the manufactured-SCM substitution route, proven bankable this year. Terra CO2's OPUS is a pozzolanic SCM made from ordinary silicate rock - https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/clean-industry/terra-co2-says-its-texas-factory-will-cut-carbon-and-cost-from-cement, not from waste, and it is the only route with documented project-level bank debt. Its first plant (240kt/yr, Cleburne, Texas) pulled that debt because Asher Materials, an Instar vehicle, signed a binding definitive agreement to acquire and operate the whole completed plant - https://www.prweb.com/releases/terra-co2-technology-announces-first-commercial-scale-plant-to-decarbonize-cement-production-signs-definitive-agreement-with-asher-materials-301924686.html, which is stronger than a tonnage take-or-pay. It broke ground in December 2025 and ships in 2027. A lender does not underwrite a molecule; it underwrites that contract.

The clever-chemistry routes stalled exactly the way policy-gated plays do. Sublime Systems runs ambient-temperature electrochemical lime, elegant physics that makes cement without a kiln at all, and it still paused its Holyoke plant and cut 10 percent of staff after the DOE clawed back its ~$87M grant - https://www.globalcement.com/news/20207-sublime-systems-pauses-holyoke-project-after-federal-funding-setback, despite a binding Microsoft offtake that is a book-and-claim attribute rather than physical parity. The lesson is uncomfortable: the more novel the binder, the higher the capex and the more it leans on a grant that can vanish.
The most elegant expression of the delete-the-kiln idea is a Cambridge one, and it is a 2027-30 bet, not a 2026 buy. Cambridge Electric Cement uses spent cement paste as the lime flux in a steel EAF - https://cambridgeelectriccement.com/, so recycled clinker comes out of a furnace that already exists. Its Cement 2 Zero consortium has run the mechanism at CELSA's 150-tonne EAF in Cardiff. But it sits on a July-2024 £2.25M seed, has no signed offtake and no cement standard for EAF-derived clinker, and its "no significant added cost" is a modelled claim from a Nature paper - https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/cement-recycling, not an audited commercial figure. Best UK science; not yet financeable.
Chart 2. A rising share of a shrinking pie. US fly ash tonnage is falling while the use-rate climbs, so the waste-SCM moat is depleting.
The one clause this analysis rewrote is the moat: abundant feedstock beats waste logistics. The earlier assumption was that the durable edge was locking up cheap waste ash. The feedstock math killed that. The scarce legacy SCMs are depleting: US fly ash fell to 63.6 Mt in 2024 from a ~130 Mt peak while beneficial use hit 72 percent - https://acaa-usa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/News-Release-Coal-Ash-Production-and-Use-2024.pdf, a rising share of a shrinking pie, and UK GGBS lost its domestic source when Port Talbot's last blast furnace closed in September 2024 - https://research.senedd.wales/research-articles/one-year-on-how-has-the-blast-furnaces-closing-affected-port-talbot/. So a startup aggregating scarce waste is buying a depleting input whose scarcity premium invites a rock or clay competitor. The durable seat is a manufactured SCM from abundant feedstock plus process IP plus a binding offtake.
"In cement, novelty is a bankability liability. The boring additive that drops into an existing grinding line and rides a signed offtake is the one a bank will finance."
The verdict of INQ-022
Honesty on the disconfirming checks: the financial-first case rests on unverified pricing and a still-advancing rival science. No audited $/t exists for OPUS or LC3, and "parity with fly ash" flatters the claim because fly ash is scarcity-inflated. Against GGBS at $70-100 the manufactured SCMs are not cost leaders. The novel-binder lane is also still moving: a UBC electrochemical belite cement in ACS Energy Letters (May 2026) claims up to a 98 percent CO2 cut while co-producing hydrogen - https://techxplore.com/news/2026-05-electricity-cement-carbon-footprint.html, and Brimstone is building an 80kt/yr Portland-from-rock plant despite losing a $189M DOE grant, on an Amazon offtake - https://www.fastcompany.com/91384600/climate-tech-startup-brimstone-just-lost-a-189-million-doe-grant-but-its-building-its-first-plant-anyway. The "not a novel binder" moat is safe for 2026-30, not forever.

The only route with real project bank debt on a binding offtake is the least novel one.
The investable layer is the manufactured-SCM integrator with a lender-grade offtake, not the materials-IP inventor and not the waste-ash aggregator. Most observers still rank cement-decarb by how clever the chemistry is; the smart-money version of that error is to race for the last cheap waste ash. The mispriced thing is neither. It is the contract-and-certification machine that turns a plant into project-financeable infrastructure, which is exactly what Terra demonstrated and what Cambridge Electric Cement still lacks. Buy the offtake, not the binder.

Long view
AI-datacentre power is becoming the organising principle of the transition. The reward goes to real physics plus real offtake, and 2026's bankable decarbonisation stories all ride infrastructure that already exists rather than the cleverest new material. Cement is the same story in a different sector.
Where cement sits in the whole system: it is the clearest test of a rule the rest of the transition is now learning the hard way. A decarbonisation product only finances if it bolts onto assets and contracts that already exist. Firm power rides existing grid interconnections and datacentre offtakes; low-carbon cement rides existing grinding lines and construction offtakes. The novelty premium that once attracted grants now repels lenders, and CBAM's live ~EUR75/tCO2 makes any lower-clinker product more valuable without gating it on a subsidy.
Contrarian take
Both camps are wrong. The "clever chemistry wins" crowd and the "lock up cheap waste ash before it runs out" crowd are backing the same losing structure. The cleverer the binder, the higher the capex and the more it leans on a grant that gets clawed back. And the waste-ash moat is a trap: fly ash and slag are depleting as coal retires and blast furnaces convert to EAF, so their rising scarcity premium is precisely what makes an abundant-feedstock manufactured SCM the financial-first buy. It is CRH, not a startup, that can afford to own the last of the ash.
