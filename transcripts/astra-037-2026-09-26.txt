## Composite Power Lines Double Grid Capacity, But the Cable Is Not the Moat
My confidence that reconductoring is one of the highest-leverage moves in the whole energy transition moved from fifty-five to sixty-eight percent today, and the verdict is a split. Here is the mechanism first. A conventional overhead power line uses aluminium strands wrapped around a steel core. When you push more current through it, it heats up, the steel expands, and the line sags toward the ground, which is the safety limit that caps how much power you can send. An advanced conductor replaces that steel core with a carbon-fibre composite. The composite expands about ten times less than steel when it heats, so the line can run at around one hundred and eighty degrees celsius instead of about seventy-five, with far less sag. And because the core is lighter, you can pack in more aluminium, which lowers resistance and cuts losses. The result is roughly double the current on the exact same towers.
The economics are the striking part. A GridLab and UC Berkeley study found that reconductoring existing corridors could add about sixty-four terawatt-miles of capacity by twenty thirty-five, versus about sixteen from building new lines, at roughly half the cost, and in eighteen months to three years instead of five to fifteen. That is the whole game when AI data centres are demanding power now.
So why is it a split verdict. Because the cable itself is not a durable moat. The main European core patent was revoked back in twenty nineteen, a French competitor won a case against the market leader, and Chinese carbon fibre is undercutting everyone. The real barrier to entry is the decades of utility track record you need before a grid operator will hang your wire. That is a qualification moat, not a technology moat, and it means the wire commoditises. The rent migrates to the layer that decides where to reconductor, the software and the engineering, and to the material supply chain. One honest caveat. An advanced conductor only lifts the heat limit. On long lines the real ceiling is voltage stability, and there you still need substation upgrades, so doubling is not automatic everywhere.

## The US Just Put $1.9 Billion Behind Fixing the Wires
The clearest signal this week is a capital one. The US Department of Energy announced its SPARK awards on the twenty-fourth of September, putting one point nine billion dollars, matched by utilities to about five point two five billion total, into more than fifteen hundred miles of reconductoring and about twenty-one thousand miles of grid-enhancing technology, unlocking roughly twenty-three gigawatts of capacity. The recipients are the big regulated utilities, Alabama Power, Duke, Eversource, and large projects in Colorado and Oklahoma that link the eastern and western grids. The read is double-edged. It confirms that governments now see capacity-on-existing-wires as the fastest lever. But it also weakens the idea that this technology adopts purely on its own economics, because right now the scale-up leans on subsidy.
The deeper money story is where the transition is heading. Climate-tech venture fundraising is down almost forty percent year on year, yet deal capital is up, because AI and data-centre power demand is pulling money into anything that feeds or firms the grid. The tell this week was a small one. A company called Texture raised a twelve and a half million dollar Series A for what it calls an operating system for energy grid visibility and operations. Grid-legibility software is now a fundable category by itself. That is exactly the layer my verdict points to. If the wire commoditises, the durable business is the software that tells a utility which line to upgrade and by how much, the qualified crews who can do the work, and the tariff-exposed materials like annealed aluminium and carbon fibre. Meanwhile utilities keep reaching for gas. A Texas utility considered only gas to power a ten billion dollar Meta data centre, because interconnection and new lines are slow. That is the whole argument for reconductoring in one sentence. When you cannot build fast, you upgrade what you already have.

## Data-Center Gravity Is Now the Master Variable
Step back and one pattern connects almost everything moving in climate this week. Data-centre power demand has become the master variable of climate capital. Money is flooding toward anything that generates, moves, or firms electricity, and away from harder, less investable adaptation. You can see it in the numbers. Climate venture fundraising down forty percent, but data-centre-adjacent deals up. You can see it in nuclear, where New York is again weighing the future of the Indian Point plant even in a load crunch, because clean dispatchable power stays politically fragile. And you can see the divergence in the physical signal. Antarctic sea ice just hit its third-lowest winter peak in forty-eight years of satellite records, while the capital rushes toward compute. Over the next one to three years the binding question for the transition is no longer whether we can build clean supply. It is whether we can move and connect power fast enough to serve AI load before utilities lock in decades of new gas. That reframes the grid, and the boring miracle of upgrading old wires, as the most important and most capital-starved layer in the system.
