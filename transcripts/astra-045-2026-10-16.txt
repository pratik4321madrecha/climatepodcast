## In Ceramic Electrochemistry, the UK's Moat Is a Licence, Not a Factory
Here is the verdict first: confirmed-split, confidence sixty-two percent, that in ceramic fuel cells and electrolysers the durable, financeable UK seat is the asset-light intellectual-property licensing model, not cell manufacturing. Ceres Power, the Imperial College spinout, is the archetype. It builds no volume cells. It licenses its solid-oxide stack designs to Denso, Delta, Weichai, Thermax and Doosan, and earns licence fees plus, now, its first royalties from Doosan's factory. The result is a gross margin around seventy-one to seventy-nine percent, the kind of margin a factory never sees.

That is the technology-and-money case for one side of the split. Now the disconfirming side, which is why this is not a clean confirm. Ceres' September interims showed revenue of twenty-two-point-eight million pounds, a loss of fifteen-point-four million, a gross margin that slipped from seventy-nine to seventy-one percent, and crucially no new licensee signed in twenty twenty-six. The business funded itself this year with a ninety-nine-million-pound equity raise, not with royalties. Asset-light and high-margin, yes; self-sustaining, not yet. And the model is partner-concentrated: Bosch walked away in February twenty twenty-five to back a rival chemistry, erasing a flagship relationship overnight.

The second leg is the materials frontier. Protonic-ceramic cells run at four to six hundred degrees instead of seven to eight-fifty, which means cheaper steels and less degradation. A twenty twenty-six Nature Communications paper reported one-and-a-half watts per square centimetre in fuel-cell mode and two amps per square centimetre in electrolysis at six hundred degrees, with over seven hundred hours of stability. Impressive, and genuinely new. But it is a single cell, watts-scale, under a thousand hours. High-temperature solid-oxide electrolysis already ships at gigawatt-per-year scale from Bloom and Topsoe. The low-temperature frontier is a three-to-five-year watch item, not something that reprices the incumbents before twenty twenty-eight.

The investable reading: underwrite the licence cadence and the royalty inflection, not the stack. The signal to watch is whether Ceres signs its one licence a year and whether Doosan royalties actually ramp.

## The Rest of the Field: Compute Is Writing the Energy Cheques
The wider field moved around one gravity well this fortnight: AI compute is now the marginal buyer of firm clean power. Google signed its largest-ever US power deal with Constellation, about three-point-six gigawatts across the PJM grid with four-point-three billion dollars committed to uprate eleven nuclear reactors. Demand, not subsidy, is setting the pace, and the same interconnection wall is live for UK AI Growth Zones and Cambridge's own compute ambitions.

Reversible fuel cells drew real money: Germany's Reverion raised a hundred-seventy-five-million-dollar Series B for plants that both generate and store in one box, and Ceres' partner Doosan signed a sixty-million-pound supply deal with Reverion, which is the exact value chain today's deep dive examined.

Transport gave a clean natural experiment on policy. US electric-vehicle sales fell thirty-nine percent year-on-year in the third quarter after the federal credit expired, while the UK posted its strongest-ever battery-electric month in September, ninety-nine thousand registrations, up thirty-six percent. Remove the subsidy and the market stalls; keep a supply-side mandate and it climbs.

The cautionary arc is the cleantech shakeout. The battery recycler Ascend Elements burned through nine hundred million dollars and filed Chapter Eleven after a federal grant was cancelled; Redwood Materials pivoted to grid storage and cut staff. Grants and offtake, not chemistry, are what break these companies. And the capital picture overall is a barbell: first-half climate funding rose fifty-five percent to twenty-six billion dollars, but deal count fell to a five-year low, with a third of all dollars going to low-carbon data centres. For a UK vantage, the money favours deep-tech hardware with a path to industrial scale and public-capital de-risking.
