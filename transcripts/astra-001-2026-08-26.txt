## Why the Material Is the Cheap Part
My confidence that the caloric material captures the rent fell again today, from twenty eight to twenty four percent, while the opposite belief, that the value sits in the integrated system, is now confirmed at around seventy eight. Here is the evidence. No company publishes a cost per kilowatt for a caloric cooler, so I estimated it from the disclosed mass and power. The active caloric material comes to roughly eight to twenty dollars per kilowatt of cooling. That is trivially cheap. The built system is north of five hundred dollars per kilowatt, and its measured efficiency, its coefficient of performance, sits between one point two and two point two, below an ordinary propane fridge at nearly four. To be a rational purchase for reasons that have nothing to do with climate, a caloric cooler needs to come in under about a hundred and eighty dollars per kilowatt and beat a coefficient of performance of three, at the same time. The material clears the cost bar with room to spare. The system clears neither. So the entire economic problem lives downstream of the material, in the actuators, the heat transfer, and the qualification. The market has already voted on this. Carrier, the air conditioning giant, put fifteen million euros into an elastocaloric company called Exergyn, valuing it above two hundred million euros. It bought equity in the integrated company, not a licence to the alloy. And the one caloric product with a real customer today is a magnetic cooling cabinet from a German firm called MagnoTherm, running fifteen percent below propane, now going into REWE supermarkets. A cabinet, not a molecule.
