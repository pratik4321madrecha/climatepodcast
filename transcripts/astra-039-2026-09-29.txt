## The UK Deep-Tech Week: Cambridge Invents, the State and Abroad Scale
Start with the map, across three sectors, Cambridge first, then London, then the rest of the UK.

In quantum and chips, Cambridge had the loudest fortnight. Here is how the three connect. All of them now run on the same two inputs: compute and materials. Artificial intelligence for materials and quantum simulation are chasing the same molecular-design prize from two directions, and both run on Nvidia hardware. Materials is the choke point underneath the chip story and the climate story alike, which is exactly why the state is concentrating money on materials and autonomous labs. Where Britain is genuinely strong is quantum and materials discovery. Where it is merely loud is scale: the money keeps pointing outward.

Three terms to carry into the room this week. A machine-learned interatomic potential is a neural network that predicts the forces between atoms, so you can simulate materials at near-quantum accuracy but far faster; it's the engine under every AI-for-materials startup. Magic state distillation is the most resource-hungry step in a fault-tolerant quantum computer, the process of refining many noisy quantum states into a few clean ones. And a grid-forming inverter is a solar or battery inverter that creates and holds the grid's frequency itself, instead of following spinning generators, which is what lets a grid run on almost pure renewables.

## Where UK Wide-Bandgap Power Actually Captures Value
Confidence on the UK power-electronics seat eased from sixty to fifty-eight percent today. The mechanism is more confirmed than before, but the investable claim weakened again. Here's the belief.

Wide-bandgap semiconductors, gallium nitride and silicon carbide, switch faster and hotter than silicon, so they shrink power supplies and cut losses. That physics is settled, and it is exactly why the bare chip is now a commodity. The proof: Taiwan Semiconductor is exiting gallium-nitride foundry by July twenty twenty-seven, and Navitas, the integration-branded pure-play, posted a nought-point-four percent gross margin last quarter. Moving off the bare die did not, by itself, protect margin.

So the question is which seat off the chip a UK team can defend, and the honest answer is: less than I hoped. There are three seats. System architecture, where the eight-hundred-volt data-centre rack lives, is owned by Nvidia and the primes, and the socket already lists fourteen chip suppliers, none of them British. Integration intellectual property is the best UK seat, and Cambridge GaN Devices holds it, but in June it signed a deal with NXP in which the prime builds the system around the Cambridge company's chip. That hands the durable rent to the prime. The third seat is the raw material. Gallium is a genuine choke point, China controls over eighty percent of it, and Western buyers pay roughly ten times the Chinese domestic price. But that is heavy industry, it isn't British, and no founder can own it.

The implication for an investor is specific. Back a Cambridge GaN Devices type company as an acquisition, priced for absorption into a prime's data-centre power roadmap, not as a standalone champion. The biggest prize, the electric-vehicle traction inverter, stays silicon carbide through at least twenty twenty-eight: nine hundred and twenty thousand eight-hundred-volt vehicles shipped last quarter, every one on silicon carbide, zero on gallium nitride. The tradeable British position is upstream, the integration intellectual property and the South Wales compound-semiconductor cluster, which primes and states buy, rather than a chip fab the UK does not have.

## The Wider Field: The Constraint Moves From Invention to Build-Out
Five threads from the wider field, and one pattern connecting them.

DeepMind released a weather model that forecasts hourly and predicts wind at turbine height, which pushes artificial intelligence weather from research into how renewables actually get planned and traded. The UK put twelve-point-six million pounds into the materials layer under quantum, masers and single-atom doping, an upstream position rather than a qubit-count headline. A UK and United States fusion pact paired the two countries on plasma modelling and on writing the regulatory rulebook first. Innovate UK opened a three-million-pound competition specifically for storage that lasts more than one hundred hours, an admission that a wind-heavy grid needs a different chemistry. And UK Power Networks warned that Britain's biggest planned AI supercomputer may wait until the early-to-mid twenty thirties for enough grid power.

The pattern is the same one the deep dive hit. The binding constraint on the energy and technology transition is shifting from invention to physical build-out and control: grid capacity, storage duration, foundry and material sovereignty, and who sets the standards. Britain reliably invents the science. The recurring risk is the funding cliff and the grid lag that let others build what it invents.
