## Why Iron Wins the Hundred-Hour Slot, and Why That Isn't the Same as Bankable
The verdict split in two directions today, and confidence moved from fifty-five to sixty percent. Start with the product, because the product is settled. An iron-air battery stores energy by rusting iron on purpose. You run current through iron and it takes on oxygen from the air to become rust, which charges it. Reverse the current and the rust gives the oxygen back, which discharges it. Iron is the cheapest abundant metal on Earth, so the energy is cheap. The number that matters is about twenty dollars per kilowatt-hour of stored energy, against two-hundred-fifty to four-hundred dollars for lithium. At four hours of duration lithium wins easily. At a hundred hours, lithium's energy cost is prohibitive and iron wins outright. The cross-over is around seven hours. Past that, iron is cheaper per kilowatt of firm capacity.
The catch is round-trip efficiency. Iron-air gives back only about forty to fifty percent of the electricity you put in. For daily trading that is fatal. For rare, deep backup, when you are covering a multi-day wind lull, it barely matters, because you are paying for the energy capacity, not for the round trip. That is the whole thesis: at long duration, dollars per kilowatt-hour dominate efficiency.
Here is what makes it more than a lab story. Three named buyers are paying directly on cost, with no subsidy. Google is funding thirty gigawatt-hours in Minnesota and paying for the grid upgrades itself. Crusoe signed twelve gigawatt-hours for AI data centers. Georgia Power signed a definitive fifteen-megawatt deal. Form Energy's backlog went from about twenty gigawatt-hours to eighty in a single year.
Now the other direction. The word bankable is doing too much work. The pure iron chemistry is not, by itself, a business. The all-iron flow leader, ESS, reported quarterly revenue of a hundred and twenty-eight thousand dollars and has been fighting to stay solvent. Eos ships a real backlog at minus seventy-one percent gross margin. Ambri went through bankruptcy. Powin, which had seventeen gigawatt-hours deployed, filed last year. Deployment scale is not a solvency signal in this field. Bankability lives inside one company's order book, underwritten by hyperscaler balance sheets, not in the metal.

## The Whole Grid Is Being Rewired Around Firm Power for AI
Step back from iron for a moment, because the same force runs through the whole field this week. Hyperscalers are now buying firm, carbon-free power directly, across every dispatchable technology at once. Google signed a three-hundred-ninety-six-megawatt geothermal deal with Fervo, its largest yet, with an option toward a gigawatt. Crusoe, the company buying twelve gigawatt-hours of iron-air, raised three billion dollars at a thirty-billion-dollar valuation. The buyer of firm capacity is no longer the utility. It is the AI cloud, and it is paying on reliability, not on policy.
Underneath that, a deliberate tilt away from lithium is taking shape. China restored a two-percent tax on lithium-ion cells from the first of September, but exempted sodium-ion and solid-state through twenty-twenty-eight, a twenty-eight-month cost head start engineered by the largest battery market on Earth. The same week, Columbia published an earth-abundant potassium-sodium-sulfur cell holding seventy-one percent capacity over a thousand cycles. The cheap-materials thesis is being subsidised and proven at the same time. Where this points over the next two years: firmness is the product, and abundant chemistries, sodium, manganese, iron, are being pushed to displace a lithium monoculture.
