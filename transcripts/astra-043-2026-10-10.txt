## The UK Deep-Tech Week: Quantum Money, AI Materials, and the Grid
Here is the state of the UK deep-tech ecosystem this week, across three sectors, Cambridge first, then London, then the rest of the country.

Start with quantum, chips and photonics, because that is where the money is. Oxford Quantum Circuits closed Europe's largest ever private quantum round earlier this year, two hundred and sixty million pounds, about three hundred and forty nine million dollars. In Cambridge, Riverlane, which builds the error-correction layer that other quantum machines plug into, opened a United States headquarters in Maryland in September. In London, Quantum Motion, a spin-qubit company that makes quantum chips on the same three-hundred-millimetre lines as ordinary silicon, extended its Series C in early September and promoted a new chief product officer. The government has put as much as two billion pounds behind national quantum over the coming decade, and Imperial opened a twelve-point-six million pound photonics and quantum programme. The pattern is clear. British quantum is no longer a lab story. It is a scaling story.

Now AI for science and materials. The anchor remains CuspAI, the Cambridge company that raised four hundred and fifty million dollars in July at a two-point-six billion dollar valuation, and then spent it not on a bigger model but on an AI Materials Foundry, a network of more than forty-five chemical and chip companies that can actually make and test what the model designs. That is the tell of the whole sector. The model is becoming a commodity. The value is moving to proprietary data, to synthesis and qualification, and to who has the offtake. CamGraPhIC, a Cambridge graphene-photonics spinout, won European clearance for a grant worth over two hundred million euros, the largest single grant ever made to a Cambridge spinout.

Climate and energy is quieter on headline raises but moving structurally. In London, Isometric, which certifies carbon removal and now industrial emissions, expanded its Series A to fifty million dollars with the Intercontinental Exchange joining. The UK government launched the Great British Grid in late September to speed up grid connections, which is the real bottleneck now that four-hour battery costs have hit a record low of seventy-eight dollars per megawatt-hour.

Three terms to carry into any of those rooms this week. First, density functional theory, or DFT, the physics-first computer method that predicts how a material behaves from the quantum behaviour of its electrons, with no experiment needed. It is what people mean when they say materials from first principles. Second, a spin qubit, a quantum bit stored in the spin of a single electron on a silicon chip, the approach that lets quantum processors ride the existing chip industry. Third, eight hundred volt HVDC, the high-voltage direct-current power architecture now being built into AI data-centre racks because the old lower-voltage wiring cannot carry a megawatt into a single rack without melting. Remember that last one. It is the thread into today's deep dive.

## Where the UK Actually Makes Money in Wide-Bandgap Chips
The question today was where the United Kingdom actually makes money in wide-bandgap power electronics, and the answer settled at seventy percent confidence, up from sixty-six. Wide-bandgap means gallium nitride and silicon carbide, two materials that switch electrical power faster and with less loss than ordinary silicon. The verdict is that the money is not in the chip itself. It is in integration, intellectual property and epitaxy, and even there it is an acquisition play, not a standalone business.

Start with the physics and the product, because that is what decides it. The bare die is commoditising on both chemistries. Silicon-carbide six-inch wafers that fetched about fifteen hundred dollars now sell for around five hundred, a two-thirds fall. The fastest-growing power-semiconductor pure-play, Navitas, reported revenue of ten and a half million dollars last quarter, down twenty-seven percent year on year, and it is still not profitable on a standard accounting basis even as it pivots hard into eight-hundred-volt data-centre power. So the device works, but making the device is not where the margin lives.

Now the three 2026 facts that settle it. First, when a large chipmaker, NXP, wanted Cambridge GaN Devices' smart-integration technology in June, it signed a collaboration, not a premium buyout. Second, Infineon won two more court rulings against its Chinese rival Innoscience in Munich, which tells you the real battleground is patents. Third, raw gallium, the feedstock for gallium nitride, hit a record Western spot price of about eighteen hundred and fifty dollars a kilogram after China cut exports by ninety-four percent. The die gets cheaper. The raw material chokes.

So the implication for an investor. The fundable British seat is the integration and epitaxy cluster, Cambridge GaN Devices, the Welsh compound-semiconductor cluster, the new Semiconductor Catapult. But you underwrite it as a trade sale into a larger chipmaker's AI-power roadmap, not as a durable franchise, because the big players absorb every independent off-die layer. And the demand that matters right now is the data-centre rack at eight hundred volts, not the electric-vehicle traction inverter, which stays silicon carbide through about 2028. The one seat no founder can own is the raw-material chokepoint itself.

## The Transition's Capital Is Splitting in Two
Step back to the wider field, because three arcs connect this week. The first is that climate and deep-tech capital is splitting into a barbell. Frontier energy and quantum are pulling mega-rounds, fusion company Helion at around four hundred and sixty-five million dollars, Oxford Quantum Circuits at three hundred and forty-nine, while the mid-stage middle starves. The second arc is policy hardening in Europe. The European Union made a ninety percent emissions cut by 2040 legally binding, though it built in a five percent international-offset escape hatch, and the UK locked its seventh carbon budget at five hundred and thirty-five million tonnes. The third arc is the opposite in the United States. BlocPower, which financed heat-pump retrofits for low-income homes and raised about two hundred and forty million dollars, is winding down after federal electrification funding was frozen. The lesson is blunt. Demand built on subsidy evaporates when the subsidy does.

One science note worth holding. In Nature Energy in late September, a nickel-hydroxide electrochemical cell for direct air capture ran a small stack for five thousand hours at about one hundred and thirty-two kilojoules per mole of carbon dioxide, with a projected path below one hundred dollars a tonne. Electrochemical carbon capture is moving from laboratory coupons toward real stacks. The long view is that the binding constraint of the transition is shifting from cost, where batteries are already cheap, to systems, the grid and the power to feed AI, which is exactly where the next three years of value and policy fights will sit.
