## UK deep tech's fortnight: quantum writes the biggest cheque, and the AI-materials model gets given away
The hardest money in UK deep tech this fortnight went to quantum hardware, not to artificial intelligence. Universal Quantum raised more than one hundred million dollars in what it calls the largest Series A ever by a UK-headquartered quantum company, co-led by DCVC and Firgun Ventures. Days earlier the National Quantum Computing Centre and the National Physical Laboratory switched on a Quantum Standards Network, bringing the British Standards Institution and the national cyber security centre into one coordinating body. That combination, a nine-figure round plus national standards, is institutional depth. It is the sort of thing that is hard to fake with a press release.

Start in Cambridge. The city's quantum names kept moving. Riverlane, the error-correction company, opened a United States headquarters in Maryland to get its technology closer to American machines. Quantinuum, the trapped-ion full-stack company with Cambridge roots, signed a cloud-access partnership in Australia. In climate, a Cambridge spinout called Reclinker raised ten million pounds to make low-carbon cement by recovering old cement paste in an electric arc furnace, with no kiln and no fresh limestone. Clean Growth Fund and Cambridge Enterprise backed it.

Move to London. The biggest artificial-intelligence-for-science number of the fortnight was Isomorphic Labs, the DeepMind drug-design spinout, reportedly in talks at a forty-billion-dollar valuation. Note that this is Alphabet capital, not a home-grown British raise. A smaller London round tells you more about where value sits: Metris Energy took five million dollars, with Octopus Ventures, to build a single data layer for renewable energy assets.

Across the rest of the United Kingdom, the government launched Great British Grid to clear a connection queue clogged with more than three hundred gigawatts of speculative projects. And a Liverpool spinout, Matter Machine, raised four million pounds led by IP Group, then hired a co-founder of Oxford Nanopore to run commercialisation. Remember that hire. It is the whole argument of today's edition.

Three terms to carry into any room this week. First, a metal-organic framework, or MOF: a crystalline material full of microscopic pores, like a molecular sponge, used to trap specific molecules such as forever chemicals or carbon dioxide. Second, an offtake agreement: a contract where a buyer commits in advance to purchase future output, which is what turns a clever material into a bankable business. Third, a trapped-ion qubit: a single charged atom held still by electric fields and controlled with lasers, one of the leading ways to build a quantum computer and the approach Universal Quantum and Quantinuum both use.

## The AI-materials model is not the moat, because the companies are giving the model away
Our confidence that the generative model is not where the money lands in artificial-intelligence materials discovery rose this session from seventy to seventy-six percent, and the stronger version of the claim nearly doubled in strength. Here is why. We went looking for a single AI-materials company that earns recurring, high-margin revenue from licensing its model. We found none.

Instead we found the opposite. Microsoft released its materials-generating model, MatterGen, under a free open licence, with no paid service behind it. Meta open-sourced a data set of more than one hundred million physics calculations and the models trained on it. A Cambridge professor put it plainly: the open data release matters more than the model itself. When the biggest labs give the model away, the model cannot be the asset.

So where does the value go? To three places. To proprietary experimental data, the real-world measurements a model is only as good as. To the synthesis and qualification path, actually making the material and proving it works. And to offtake, the chemical or chip company that buys the finished product. Materials Nexus, a British company, says it plainly on its own site: it licenses its intellectual property and earns royalties, while manufacturing partners scale production. It designs. Someone else builds and sells.

The clearest tell came this fortnight. Matter Machine, the Liverpool spinout, raised its first institutional money and immediately hired a seasoned commercial leader to run go-to-market. A pure software business does not lead with that hire. A business that knows the value is in the deal, the data and the customer does.

The investment implication for someone building toward climate and deep-tech venture in Britain: do not back the best model. Back whoever owns the data, locks the scale-up partner, and holds the offtake, or the commercial operator who assembles those three. One honest gap remains. We still cannot see who owns the data generated inside these new industry consortia, the platform or the member that ran the experiment. That is the question that decides the next round of this inquiry.

## The field keeps moving value off the clever part and onto the layer that captures it
Step back and the whole field is telling one story: value keeps sliding off the clever component and onto the layer that captures it. Institutional climate money is consolidating, not retreating. TPG closed a second climate fund at around ten billion dollars, up from seven, even against American political headwinds. The money is pooling into fewer, larger managers.

Demand for electric cars is reaccelerating where it counts. BYD sold a record two hundred and seventy-three thousand battery-electric cars in a single month, up a third on last year, even as its total slipped. Industrial heat storage is crossing from demonstration to deployment: a French company, Epyr, raised fifteen million euros and switched on its first commercial unit at a paper mill, where hot bricks now supply a quarter of the site's steam. And agricultural methane is becoming an investable measurement business, with a Paris company raising seven million euros to track rice-paddy emissions from satellites.

The long view. The binding constraints of this transition keep moving from cost to systems, and from the clever element to the layer that owns it. Batteries already hit record-low prices, so the number stopped being the story. The AI-materials model is being open-sourced before it ever earned a licence. And in the grid, the bottleneck is the connection queue, not the generation. For the next year or two, follow the data, the qualification path, the offtake and the grid connection. That is where the rent lives.
