## Why "Own the Layer Off the Die" Just Got Harder
Confidence on the power-electronics moat thesis moved from sixty-four to sixty percent today, and the strong form stayed weak at about thirty-five. Here is the correction. Two sessions ago we established that the wide-bandgap transistor die is now a commodity on both chemistries, so the durable value must have migrated off it  -  to system architecture, to integration IP, and to the raw-material chokepoint. That direction still holds. What this session interrogated is whether an independent company can actually own those off-die seats. The answer is: much less than the thesis assumed.
The hardest single fact is Navitas. In the second quarter of 2026 Navitas posted a GAAP gross margin of zero-point-four percent on ten and a half million dollars of revenue, and this is the archetypal integration-branded player with a fifty-percent year-on-year surge in high-power. Moving off the bare die into the integration layer did not, by itself, protect margin. The only player holding above fifty percent gross margin is Power Integrations, and it does so as a scaled incumbent with its own fab and its own IP  -  not as a fabless startup.
And the incumbents are internalising the seat directly. Infineon shipped its own integrated CoolGaN half-bridge driver in February, then acquired GaN Systems and C2i Semiconductors for AI-data-centre power. Onsemi bought the vertical-GaN leader NexGen out of bankruptcy for about twenty million dollars. Every independent off-die leader that mattered got bought, not scaled. The substrate chokepoint tells the same story: gallium-oxide six-inch wafers only begin sampling this year, mass production is 2029, and the material conducts heat about forty times worse than silicon carbide  -  so the one productised part above twelve hundred volts is a two-amp prototype with no disclosed price. The under-priced implication: the venture-accessible seat here is real, but it is exit-shaped. You invest to be acquired into a prime's roadmap, not to build a franchise.

## Where the Money Actually Rotated This Week
The wider sweep this week says one thing loudly: AI compute is eating climate capital. First-half climate-tech venture funding hit twenty-six billion dollars, up fifty-five percent year on year, but it is narrow  -  low-carbon data-centre power was thirty-four percent of the half, up from about three percent a year earlier, the top ten deals took over forty percent of the money, and deal count actually fell about a quarter. The money is concentrating on clean electrons fast enough for AI, which is exactly why the in-rack power-conversion layer we studied today matters. Alongside it: grid congestion cost the US a record seventeen billion dollars last year, and Google, NVIDIA and Anthropic all backed Emerald AI to make data centres flex their draw so they can interconnect at all. The grid, not generation, is the binding constraint, and the layers around demand are where the rent is moving.
