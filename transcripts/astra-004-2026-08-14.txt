## Why the Reconductoring Money Goes to Software, Not the Wire
We're closing the advanced-conductor inquiry at confirmed, with confidence at eighty-three percent, up from eighty. Reconductoring means re-stringing an existing transmission tower with a carbon-fibre composite core instead of a steel one. The carbon core expands about fourteen times less than aluminium when it heats up, so the same tower can run at nearly two hundred degrees without the line sagging into the trees. That carries around twice the current at lower loss. The product works. It costs roughly one thousand two hundred and fifty dollars per extra megawatt-mile against two thousand for a new line, and it energizes in eighteen months to three years instead of five to ten. None of that is in doubt.
What the last session settled is the money. First, the thing that unlocks it. The whole industry is waiting on a one-point-nine-billion-dollar federal grant program called SPARK to name winners. It still hasn't. And it doesn't matter, because the real teeth already arrived quietly somewhere else. The Department of Energy closed a three-point-two-six-billion-dollar loan to AEP Texas in July, on top of a one-point-six-billion loan a year earlier, both explicitly for reconductoring. That's nearly five billion dollars of closed debt to regulated utilities. Underneath it, eight states and counting have passed laws forcing utilities to evaluate advanced conductors in their planning. A fix spread across debt and a dozen statehouses has no single point of failure. It's more durable than the one grant everyone is refreshing their browser for.
Second, where the durable profit sits. Not in the cable. The core is a commodity now, a patent was revoked, and the two firms that wrote the qualification standard are buying up the supply. So the money moves to installation and certification. Here's the twist that settles it for us: in Britain, even installation is taken. National Grid handed its one-point-two-billion-pound reconductoring programme to five construction giants in June. Certification is a near-monopoly held by one Canadian lab. So the only seat left for a newcomer is asset-light. It's the software that decides how hard to run the line, and the independent testing lab that doesn't yet exist in the UK. The wire is plumbing. The intelligence on top of it is the opportunity.

## The Data-Centre Boom Is Now the Climate-Capital Story
Step back to the whole field, and one pattern dominates. Climate funding in the first half of this year hit twenty-six billion dollars, up fifty-five percent, but the number of deals fell to a five-year low. The money is concentrating into fewer, bigger cheques, and a third of it now goes to data-centre power. Antora raised five hundred and fifty million dollars for thermal batteries, which store heat in blocks of carbon and give it back as industrial heat or power. Commonwealth Fusion raised another billion. Both are firm-power bets financed on data-centre demand, not on decarbonisation.
Underneath that boom is a split worth watching. Private mega-rounds are setting records while grant-dependent factories collapse. In the same fortnight, the green-cement firm Sublime laid off two-thirds of its staff after a federal grant was cancelled, a battery recycler filed for bankruptcy, and an indoor-farming company that raised four hundred million dollars shut down. The lesson repeats across sectors: the fragile layer is domestic manufacturing that depends on a subsidy. The durable layer is demand-pulled and asset-light. And the clearest bet on where artificial intelligence actually creates climate value, materials discovery, is being led from Cambridge, by CuspAI, now valued at two-point-six billion dollars.
