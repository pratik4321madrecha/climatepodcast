## AI data-center cooling: why the dielectric fluid is not the moat
The heat has to go somewhere. The rent does not follow it into the coolant.
The bet that a proprietary cooling fluid becomes the AI-datacenter moat is weaker now, not stronger, and the primary documents are what moved it. NVIDIA's flagship racks and the Open Compute Project are deliberately keeping the coolant a commodity, so the durable rent is landing everywhere except the fluid: up at the cold-plate and CDU integrators booking multi-billion backlogs, and down inside the silicon as foundries etch cooling channels into the chip. The cooling technology itself passes a clean financial-first test. The fluid-as-asset thesis does not.

The Workshop · the mechanics
Start with the physics: the heat has nowhere to go but into a liquid
A rack of AI accelerators now dumps more heat than air can physically carry away. An NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 draws around 120 kW per rack - https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/analysis/nvidia-gtc-jensen-huang-data-center-rack-density/, and the Vera Rubin reference design points at roughly 600 kW. Moving air fast enough to cool that would mean gale-force fans and still-melting silicon. So a liquid has to touch the hot surface. Direct-to-chip (DTC) means water or a water-glycol mix flows through a metal cold plate bolted directly onto the GPU package, carrying heat to a coolant distribution unit (CDU) and out. Immersion means the whole board sits in a bath of electrically non-conducting dielectric fluid. Think of a cold plate as a car radiator pressed flat against the engine, and immersion as dropping the engine into the radiator.

On the merits, the technology works and it wins on money alone. Direct-to-chip drives PUE toward 1.03-1.20 and cuts five-year total cost of ownership by about a third - https://introl.com/blog/direct-to-chip-cooling-pue-below-12-implementation (PUE, power usage effectiveness, is total facility power divided by the power reaching the chips, so 1.0 is the ideal). A colocation operator buys it to fit more revenue-earning GPUs into the same power envelope. That is the financial-first test this desk applies to everything, and cooling passes it cleanly. So the question was never does it work. The question was where the durable profit lands as the whole industry goes liquid.
The thesis was that the fluid could be the moat. The evidence now says mostly not. It was tempting: as racks cross into megawatt territory, the electrically active dielectric fluid touching a $40,000 GPU looks like the component you would least want to get wrong, so its maker should earn qualification-locked rent, the way a jet-engine alloy supplier does. If an AI-discovered molecule held that position, even better. Two days of primary-document interrogation pushed confidence the other way.
The cooling wins; the fluid-as-asset claim loses. Confidence on "a proprietary fluid captures durable rent instead of the integrator or foundry" moved 32% → 26%. The gating factor is market structure, not physics: the people who set the specs are engineering the coolant into a commodity on purpose.
The dominant cooling mode runs on commodity water, by design. The GB300 NVL72 build guide lists only deionised water and 25% propylene glycol - https://lenovopress.lenovo.com/lp2357-lenovo-nvidia-gb300-nvl72-rack-scale-ai, with no NVIDIA approved-fluid matrix, and the 600 kW Vera Rubin reference rack stays on water cold plates - https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/gigawatt-ai-factories-ocp-vera-rubin. The Open Compute Project went further and wrote its cold-plate requirements as fluid properties expressly to "avoid proprietary lock-in" - https://www.opencompute.org/documents/ocp-acs-liquid-cooling-cold-plate-requirements-pdf, a specification designed to keep fluids interchangeable rather than to name a winning product. Even the UK's cooling specialist Iceotope runs a multi-vendor, coolant-agnostic approved-fluid program - https://www.iceotope.com/company/news/approved-fluid-vendor-program. Where the argument expected a warranty-tied approved-fluid list to form, the primary docs show the industry actively preventing one.

The rent hardened UP at the integrator layer, with 2026 numbers to prove it. The value sits with whoever ships the NVIDIA-certified cold-plate and manifold system, not whoever supplies the water inside it. Vertiv is guiding to roughly $14B for FY26 on a $15B-plus backlog - https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/vertiv-reports-strong-second-quarter-2026-with-diluted-eps-growth-of-53-adjusted-diluted-eps-growth-of-60-raises-full-year-2026-guidance-across-all-key-metrics-302837598.html; nVent reported a record $2.6B backlog with orders up 40% - https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/nvent-electrics-2-6b-backlog-150700015.html; and Eaton paid $9.5B for Boyd Thermal - https://www.eaton.com/us/en-us/company/news-insights/news-releases/2025/eaton-signs-agreement-to-acquire-boyd-thermal--expanding-solutio.html. A single integrator's backlog dwarfs the whole coolant market. Capital is voting for the hardware seat.
The most interesting move is DOWN: the next materials rent is draining into the chip itself. Heat removal is starting to happen inside the silicon, which lodges the future advantage with the foundry and the packaging house rather than any external fluid. TSMC has demonstrated an in-silicon micro-cooler (IMC-Si) hitting 0.055 C/W, targeting roughly 2027 - https://semiwiki.com/semiconductor-manufacturers/362017-breaking-the-thermal-wall-tsmc-demonstrates-direct-to-silicon-liquid-cooling-on-cowos/ (C/W is thermal resistance, degrees of temperature rise per watt removed, so lower is better). Microsoft, with EPFL spinout Corintis, is etching leaf-vein coolant channels into the back of the die and reports roughly 3x better heat removal than a cold plate - https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/innovation/microfluidics-liquid-cooling-ai-chips/. When the cooling channel is part of the wafer, the moat is the process node, not the liquid pumped through it.
"NVIDIA and the Open Compute Project are engineering away from fluid lock-in. The real next-generation materials bet is a cooling channel etched into the silicon, owned by the foundry."
The verdict, in one line
Even the flagship AI-discovered coolant monetises as hardware, not a molecule. The sub-prediction that the first commercial AI-designed molecule would be a GPU coolant did come true: Microsoft Discovery screened 367,000 candidates down to a non-PFAS coolant - https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/transforming-rd-with-agentic-ai-introducing-microsoft-discovery/, and Orbital Industries raised $50M with NVIDIA NVentures, shipping alongside 2027 GPUs - https://fortune.com/2026/05/28/exclusive-orbital-industries-raises-50-million-series-b-funding-round-ai-to-discover-exotic-new-materials/. But Orbital's founder is explicit that they "sell hardware devices," not a licensable molecule, and NVIDIA is an investor, not a customer or acquirer. The AI found the fluid. The business is still a bundled chiller.
One narrow fluid moat survives, and only one. Chemours signed a February-2026 qualification-locked joint development agreement with 2CRSi on its low-GWP Opteon 2P50 two-phase fluid - https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/chemours-launches-two-phase-immersion-cooling-fluid/. That is defensible chemistry with a real lock-in, but it lives in niche two-phase immersion, the mode that 3M's PFAS exit gutted rather than opened - https://www.perstorp.com/en/news_center/news/2026/february/perstorp_expands_data_center_liquid_cooling_portfolio. It is a real pond. It is not an ocean, and it is not venture-scale on its own.
This is the element-to-integration pattern with a new twist: value can migrate DOWN into the substrate as readily as UP into the system. Most observers see "3M left, PFAS is restricted, AI needs cooling" and reach for a proprietary-fluid pure-play. They are pricing a ~$0.18B pond as if it were the $3.3B-plus ocean, and missing that the ocean itself is starting to drain into the chip. The sourcing filter that follows: underwrite the qualified system and its certification authority, keep a live watch on foundry-owned in-silicon cooling, and treat any pure dielectric-fluid pitch as a niche two-phase story that must prove a durable, disclosed margin or it is not investable.
The Wide Lens · State of Climate Tech
The AI-power nexus is now the center of gravity for climate capital. H1'26 climate-tech VC hit $26.1bn, up 55%, as low-carbon datacenter tech jumped from ~3% to 34% of all climate funding and overtook energy - https://www.ctvc.co/h126-climate-tech-funding-up-55-to-26bn-thanks-to-data-centers/. Valar Atomics ($1B microreactors), Base Power ($1B distributed firming) and Antora ($550M thermal) show the money chasing firm power for compute, the same demand driver behind today's cooling story.
Grid hardware, not generation, is the binding constraint. Transformer lead times sit near four years with GSU demand up 274% since 2019 - https://www.powermag.com/transformers-in-2026-shortage-scramble-or-self-inflicted-crisis/, and the DOE deployed $375M plus invoked the Defense Production Act for grid manufacturing. The bottleneck owner wins again: a physical component nobody can make fast enough.
Green-hydrogen capitulation is spreading into the supply chain. Topsoe is hibernating its 500MW solid-oxide-electrolyser factory and scrapping its US plant in a $0.5bn-plus Power-to-X restructuring - https://www.hydrogeninsight.com/electrolysers/topsoe-to-hibernate-its-new-solid-oxide-electrolyser-factory-in-denmark-until-demand-is-robust-/2-1-1991127. Demand, not electrolyser cost, is what failed to show up.
Capital is bifurcating toward hard tech and away from consumer-green. Long-duration storage (Ore Energy's $43M iron-air) and profitable EV two-wheelers (River's $120M) pull funding while alt-protein has seen 80+ closures since Sep-24 - https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/food-tech-alternative-protein-ma-consolidation-closure-investment/, and cat-bond issuance set a record near $18bn in H1.
AI-for-materials is now feeding the hardware layer directly. CuspAI in Cambridge, Argonne's multi-agent materials discovery and Zanskar's AI geothermal siting all point the same way: the discovery engine and the physical bottleneck are converging, which is exactly how an AI-designed coolant became a real product this year.
LONG VIEW: the compute build-out is reshaping firm power, grid hardware and cooling at once, and in every one of them the durable value sits with the owner of the physical bottleneck, never the commodity input. Over the next 12-36 months the tell to watch is whether that bottleneck in cooling moves decisively into the foundry.
Where this sits in the transition: cooling is the third face of the same coin as firm power and grid hardware. The AI data-center is the demand shock, and each supporting layer, generation, transmission, and heat rejection, is being rebuilt around it. Cooling matters because it directly sets how many GPUs fit inside a fixed power and water budget, which is now the true unit of the buildout. The lesson repeats: the transition's profits accrue to whoever owns the physical chokepoint, and in cooling that owner is turning out to be the certified integrator today and the foundry tomorrow, not the fluid.
Contrarian Take
The obvious trade is backwards, and the primary documents prove it. NVIDIA (GB300 and Vera Rubin on commodity water) and OCP (a properties spec written to "avoid proprietary lock-in") are deliberately commoditising the coolant, while the next materials rent drains INTO the chip via TSMC's IMC-Si and the Microsoft/Corintis microfluidics. Anyone underwriting a pure dielectric-fluid play as the AI-cooling moat is buying a foundry story and mislabelling it as a chemistry one.
