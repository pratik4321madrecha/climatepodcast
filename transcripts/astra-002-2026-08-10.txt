## Why the AI Coolant Is Not the Moat
Confidence that a proprietary cooling fluid is the moat in AI data centres fell again today, from thirty-two percent to twenty-six percent. We went in asking whether the dielectric fluid, the liquid that carries heat away from the chip, becomes the layer where a materials company earns durable, locked-in margin. The answer, on primary evidence, is no.

Start with the physics, because the physics is real and it does force the change. An NVIDIA GB200 rack now draws about a hundred and twenty kilowatts. The next generation, Vera Rubin, is a reference design at around six hundred kilowatts per rack. Air cannot move that much heat. So liquid cooling is not a green choice, it is the only choice, and buyers adopt it purely on cost and performance. Direct-to-chip cooling, where a cold plate sits right on the processor, takes the power usage effectiveness of a data centre from about one point five down to nearly one point zero three, and cuts five-year running cost by around a third. That part of the thesis is confirmed. The demand wave is real and it needs no climate story.

Here is where the hypothesis breaks. The fluid doing that job is ordinary. NVIDIA's own rack guides for the GB300 list only two coolants a customer can pick: deionised water, and water with twenty-five percent propylene glycol, a common antifreeze. There is no approved-brand list. And the six-hundred-kilowatt Vera Rubin reference design still runs on water cold plates. The Open Compute Project, the industry hardware standards body, goes further: its cold-plate requirements specify the fluid only by its physical properties, and state in plain language that the goal is to avoid proprietary lock-in. In other words, the most important buyer and the main standards body are both actively working to keep the coolant a swappable commodity. A fluid company cannot build a toll booth on a road the toll authority is deliberately keeping open.

The implication for an investor is direct. The coolant market is about a hundred and eighty million dollars. The market for the cooling hardware around it, the cold plates and the coolant distribution units, is about three point three billion dollars, roughly eighteen times larger. Underwriting a pure coolant company as the AI-cooling moat is pricing a pond as if it were the ocean.

## Where the Cooling Money Actually Sits
Follow the money and it lands on the integrators, not the chemists. The twenty twenty-six numbers are unambiguous. Vertiv, which makes cold plates and coolant distribution units, guided its full-year revenue to about fourteen billion dollars, up thirty-seven percent, with a backlog above fifteen billion dollars and adjusted operating margins near twenty-three percent. nVent reported a record two point six billion dollar backlog with orders up about forty percent. Eaton paid nine and a half billion dollars for Boyd's thermal business to own what it calls a grid-to-chip bundle. And Ecolab, a water-treatment company, paid four and three-quarter billion dollars for CoolIT, a cold-plate maker, at around twenty-nine times earnings.

The signal in those deals is that the durable seat is the qualified system, the thing NVIDIA certifies into its reference design, not the molecule inside it. Even the one company that genuinely has an AI-designed coolant proves the point. Orbital Industries, a London startup founded by a former DeepMind scientist, raised fifty million dollars with NVIDIA's venture arm. But its founder is explicit that they do not license the molecule. In his words, "we sell those hardware devices." They ship the coolant bundled inside a refrigeration unit. NVIDIA is an investor, not a customer. So even a validated AI-discovered material earns its money as a qualified box, not as intellectual property on a shelf.

One honest caveat, because a test that only confirms itself is not a test. There is one place a proprietary fluid still holds a moat: niche two-phase immersion cooling, where the liquid actually boils to carry heat. Chemours signed a joint development deal in February with a European builder for a low-global-warming two-phase fluid after formal qualification. That is a real, defensible chemistry relationship. But it lives in a small corner of the market while the mainstream runs on commodity water.

## The Wider Field: Firm Power for Compute
Step back and today's finding sits inside the single biggest capital rotation in climate tech right now: money is pouring into anything that powers or cools an AI data centre. In the first half of twenty twenty-six, climate-tech venture funding hit twenty-six billion dollars, up fifty-five percent, and low-carbon data-centre technology jumped from about three percent of that to thirty-four percent, overtaking energy as the largest single category. This week alone, Valar Atomics raised a billion dollars for microreactors, Base Power raised a billion for distributed home batteries dispatched like a grid asset, and Antora raised five hundred and fifty million for thermal storage, all aimed at compute demand.

Underneath the money, the binding constraint has shifted from cash to hardware. Transformer lead times have stretched to about four years, and the US Department of Energy has invoked the Defense Production Act for grid equipment. The pattern rhymes with today's cooling story: the scarce, slow, physical thing is where the rent is, not the commodity input. Over the next twelve to thirty-six months the through-line is clear. The compute build-out is now the demand driver reshaping firm power, grid hardware and cooling alike, and in each one the durable value sits with whoever owns the physical bottleneck, not the raw material flowing through it.
