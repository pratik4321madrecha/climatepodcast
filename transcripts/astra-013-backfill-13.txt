THE SPINE · THESIS
An overhead line's real limit is thermal sag, not the wire's headline rating. Push more current through a conductor and resistive heating warms the metal; the metal expands, the span droops, and once the wire nears the ground or a tree the operator must throttle back. Conventional ACSR (aluminium-conductor steel-reinforced, a 1960s design where aluminium strands wrap a steel core) - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459140/ is capped near 75°C for exactly this reason. Its ampacity (the maximum continuous current before it sags past clearance) is set by how far it stretches when hot, and steel stretches a lot.
Swap the steel core for a carbon-fibre composite core and the sag problem largely disappears. This is what CTC Global's ACCC and TS Conductor's aluminium-encapsulated carbon core (AECC) - https://www.compositesworld.com/articles/composite-conductor-cores-boost-capacity-cut-cost-to-help-power-grids-meet-ai-demand do. A carbon composite core has a coefficient of thermal expansion of about 1.61×10⁻⁶/°C against aluminium's 23×10⁻⁶/°C, roughly 14x less, so the wire barely lengthens as it heats. It can therefore run continuously at 180-200°C instead of ~75°C. The core is like an aircraft-wing spar: stiff, light, and barely growing when hot, so it holds the span while the aluminium around it does the conducting.

Because the core is thinner, the conductor carries about 28% more annealed aluminium in the same diameter, and the extra metal plus the higher running temperature buys roughly double the current at 25-40% lower loss. That loss cut matters twice: an I²R loss (the resistive heat wasted as current I flows through resistance R) both wastes energy and self-limits ampacity, so a cooler-running conductor compounds its own advantage. The whole class is called HTLS (high-temperature low-sag) conductor, and composite-core is its most aggressive form.
The verdict: we asked whether restringing existing towers with composite-core conductors is a financial-first, roughly-2x-capacity upgrade, and we now believe it is, at 75% from 50%, because the physics is materials-real and the economics pencil unsubsidised. This is a rare finding that is positive on first principles rather than a green-premium that fails on cost.
Independent, non-vendor work backs the ~2x claim. A 2024 PNAS study - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459140/ found reconductoring roughly doubles line power and can meet most near-term interzonal need. The economics are what make this a system-level lever, not a niche retrofit. The GridLab / UC Berkeley 2024 techno-economic study - https://www.2035report.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/GridLab_2035-Reconductoring-Technical-Report.pdf found that because 98% of US lines are under 50 miles and thermally limited, reconductoring can supply about 90% of near-term interzonal transmission need to 2035 - https://www.utilitydive.com/news/reconductoring-power-lines-transmission-capacity-goldman-gridlab/712643/ at under half the cost and in 18 months to 3 years versus 5 to 15 for a new line, for roughly $85bn of system savings. The adoption gate is regulatory capex-bias, not physics or bankability. Regulated utilities earn a return on the CAPITAL they deploy, an approved return on equity of about 9.7% in 2024 - https://rmi.org/rebalancing-return-on-equity-to-accelerate-an-affordable-clean-energy-future/. That is the classic Averch-Johnson effect: a cheaper upgrade earns the utility LESS than an expensive new line, so the rational owner prefers the big rate-base spend. The fix is arriving in fragments: Montana HB729 grants a higher rate of return for advanced conductors; FERC Order 1920 requires planners to "consider" them (consider ≠ deploy); DOE's SPARK programme puts $1.9bn behind grid upgrades with selections in Aug-2026; and FERC's 18-Jun-2026 Section 206 show-cause hit all six RTOs over AI large-load interconnection, with about 2,300 GW stuck in US queues.
The moat is the weak leg, and honesty about it changes the investment case. The wire itself costs 2 to 4x ACSR, so the under-half-cost result rests on avoided rights-of-way and structures, not a cheaper conductor. A steel-core ACSS also reaches ~2x, so the capacity jump is not unique to composite. And at least six vendors sell the core - CTC Global (185,000+ km across 65 countries) - https://www.compositesworld.com/articles/composite-conductor-cores-boost-capacity-cut-cost-to-help-power-grids-meet-ai-demand, TS Conductor ($60M from Breakthrough Energy Ventures, NGP and NextEra plus a $134M South Carolina plant), 3M-Southwire ACCR, Prysmian, Nexans, Lamifil and Apar - so the chemistry commoditises. The durable rent sits in installation and qualification (IEC TS 62818-1, trained crews) and in the regulated asset owner, a seventh instance of value migrating from the element to integration and deployment. And reconductoring does not touch transformers (128-week lead times, the harder chokepoint), permitting, or the ~6x interregional need.
