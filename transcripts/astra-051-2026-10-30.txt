## Why Owning the Patents Beats Owning the Factory in Ceramic Fuel Cells
Confidence on this moved from sixty-seven to seventy-three percent. The question was whether the durable, financeable British seat in ceramic electrochemical cells is the asset-light patent-licensing model, Ceres Power, rather than building cells in a factory. Start with the technology. A solid-oxide cell is a ceramic sandwich that runs hot, around seven hundred to eight hundred and fifty degrees, and runs both ways: feed it electricity and water and it splits out hydrogen, that is electrolysis; feed it fuel and it makes electricity, that is a fuel cell. The newer protonic-ceramic version runs cooler, around four hundred to six hundred and fifty degrees, which should make everything around the cell cheaper. The physics is real. The problem is durability. The best large-area protonic cells this year run at good current but survive only fifty to a hundred hours, and Europe is still funding targets of just one kilowatt over two thousand hours. A bankable project needs a megawatt over five thousand hours. So the low-temperature breakthrough is proven in the lab and nowhere near commercial, and it will not reprice the incumbents before about 2028.
Now the money, and this is where it got interesting. Ceres does not build factories. It licenses its cell design and takes a royalty when partners like Doosan, Delta and Weichai build and sell. In the first half of 2026 it reported twenty-two point eight million pounds of revenue, a seventy-one percent gross margin, and a hundred and seventy-two million pounds of cash, roughly twelve years of runway. Its royalties are still tiny, about a hundred and eighty thousand pounds, and management says they scale from 2028. Meanwhile the companies that did build SOEC factories are writing them down. Topsoe is hibernating its five hundred megawatt Herning factory and cancelled a second plant, on a restructuring charge of around three point five to three point nine billion Danish kroner. Thyssenkrupp Nucera abandoned its plan to mass-produce SOEC stacks. In a market where hydrogen demand stalled, owning the factory is the liability and owning the patents is the shelter.
There is one honest counter-argument, and its name is Bloom Energy. Bloom is vertically integrated, it builds its own fuel cells, and in the third quarter it reported one point two four billion dollars of revenue, up a hundred and thirty-eight percent. So integration can out-earn licensing in absolute dollars, but only in the segment where demand is real, which right now is behind-the-meter power for data centres, not hydrogen; and Bloom does it at thirty-four percent gross margin, half of Ceres. So the honest verdict is segment-specific. In hydrogen, asset-light wins because the market is starved and factories punish you. In power, integration can win, but on half the margin with the capital risk on your own balance sheet. For a British investor, the lesson is the one this engine keeps reaching: the capturable seat is the layer that stays scarce, the intellectual property, not the factory.

## The Week the Transition Routed Through AI Power
One pattern connected most of this week's climate news: capital and demand are both routing through AI power. Venture money is forming a barbell. In the third quarter the ten largest climate rounds totalled eight point two billion dollars, and fission and fusion took four of them; mega-deals were eighty-two percent of the money even as the number of deals fell to a five-year low. Huge cheques for firm generation, a thinning middle for carbon removal and hydrogen and chemicals.
On the grid, Google and the demand-response company Voltus went to one gigawatt of virtual power plant in the most data-centre-stressed grid in America, ten times their June target. Hyperscalers are now treating demand flexibility as fast new grid capacity. On storage, a new failure database showed US grid-battery fire rates have fallen about ninety-seven percent since 2016 while deployments rose roughly two hundred and fifty times, which quietly retires the safety story that haunted the sector. On batteries, CATL put a date on automotive solid-state, twenty amp-hour cells at about five hundred watt-hours per kilogram, small-scale production targeted for 2027.
And the demand engine is reaching materials, not just electrons. Meta became an offtake buyer for about two hundred thousand tons of certificates from Fortera's low-carbon cement. The playbook that scaled clean power, a big corporate buyer de-risking the first plant, is now reaching cement. The long view: value is accruing to whoever owns the scarce, capturable layer, and the bankability blockers that haunted each new technology are being retired one dataset at a time. That is the same lesson today's ceramic deep dive reached from the other direction.
