## Why Two Industrial Aluminium Cells Both Refuse to Publish One Number
Our confidence that inert-anode smelting is a financial-first upgrade fell again today, from twenty-eight to twenty-four percent, and we closed the inquiry at thesis grade. Here is the mechanism first. A normal aluminium smelter runs electricity through dissolved alumina using a carbon block as the positive electrode. That carbon burns away into carbon dioxide as it works, so you constantly feed in new carbon and you emit about one and a half tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of metal. An inert anode replaces that carbon block with a metal or ceramic electrode that does not burn. It gives off oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. The prize is obvious. The question we were testing is narrower and harder. Would a rational smelter buy this to make money, for lower running cost and higher output, and not just to sell greener metal?
The answer, settled today, is no, and the proof is a silence. In November last year ELYSIS, the Rio Tinto and Alcoa joint venture, started the world's first commercial-size cell at four hundred and fifty thousand amps. In March this year RUSAL commissioned a second industrial line in Krasnoyarsk. Two independent industrial cells now exist. Neither one has published its current efficiency or its energy use per kilogram. Those are exactly the two numbers that would prove an operating-cost win. A company that had a cost advantage would publish them to take market share. Neither does. Nine months on, ELYSIS still says only that the cell will undergo several more years of testing.
Meanwhile the independent physics points the other way. A peer-reviewed review this year puts inert-anode current efficiency between sixty and ninety-two percent against carbon's ninety-plus, and minimum energy at about nine kilowatt-hours per kilogram against six and a quarter for the conventional cell. That is roughly forty-seven percent more energy at the floor, and the review concludes there are no definitive operating-cost savings today. And when smelters spend their own money in twenty twenty-six, they buy carbon. Emirates Global Aluminium and Sunstone just broke ground on a three hundred thousand tonne per year carbon-anode plant. So the durable model is this. Inert anodes are a green-brand compliance asset, sold on carbon intensity, not a cheaper way to make metal. The technology works. The merchant case does not, and the only tradeable seat near-term is the enabling-materials layer, the alloys and coatings, not the smelter.
Two more facts sealed it. The revenue stream that inert metal actually depends on, the green premium, is thin and does not reward what you might think. The London Metal Exchange finalised its sustainable-metals pricing methodology in February and tightened the aluminium carbon threshold from ten to eight tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of metal, but that is an assessment framework, not a liquidly traded contract, and the spot market often shows no premium at all. Worse for the thesis, the premium is benchmarked at standard P1020 purity, so it rewards carbon intensity and never purity. Inert-anode metal cannot escape commodity pricing by being cleaner. And in India, the country adding the most new smelting capacity, the government's own aluminium roadmap from January treats inert anodes as a long-horizon research item while Hindalco adds conventional carbon-anode capacity now. Everywhere the real money goes, it goes to carbon. The verdict trajectory across four sessions was fifty, thirty-eight, thirty-two, twenty-eight, and now twenty-four percent. The technology is real. The merchant story is not, and we can stop asking.

## The Largest Grid Battery Ever, and Where the Capital Concentrated
The money this fortnight tells one story. Long-duration storage found its buyer, and that buyer is the AI build-out. Form Energy, which makes iron-air batteries, meaning cells that rust and un-rust iron to store power for four days rather than four hours, now has more than seventy-five gigawatt-hours under contract. Thirty of those gigawatt-hours are a single deal with Google and Xcel, the largest grid battery ever contracted. Twelve more go to Crusoe for data centres. For two years the open question on iron-air was who pays for multi-day storage. The answer turned out to be hyperscalers securing firm power for compute.
The pattern repeats across the fortnight. First-half climate-tech funding was twenty-six billion dollars, up fifty-five percent on last year, but the number of deals fell twenty-five percent. Low-carbon data centres alone were thirty-four percent of all of it. Capital is concentrating into fewer, larger, compute-adjacent deals. Sila landed a one and a half billion dollar Pentagon loan to scale silicon-anode battery material, defence balance sheets now underwriting the battery supply chain. And the public markets thawed, with Fervo Energy's geothermal listing up thirty-five percent and X-Energy's nuclear listing up twenty-seven. Set against that, two failures worth naming. Sakowin, a French turquoise-hydrogen deep-tech, fell into liquidation when both buyers walked, and the battery recycler Ascend Elements filed Chapter Eleven. Grant-dependent and commodity-exposed plays are the ones breaking. The through-line to our aluminium verdict is the same. Capital rewards the thing with a paying customer today, not the thing with the better press release.

## AI for Science Became a Public Good This Month
The concrete climate-and-AI intersection this fortnight is that frontier science models are being given away. Google DeepMind open-sourced WeatherNext Two and a cyclone model under a permissive licence, adding about twenty-four hours of cyclone lead time, which for adaptation and insurance is a real operational gain, not a demo. Argonne National Laboratory published a multi-agent system that runs atomistic materials simulations end to end, an administrator agent orchestrating specialist agents, compressing parts of materials discovery from years to days. And CuspAI, the Cambridge company, launched what it calls an AI Materials Foundry alongside a four hundred and fifty million dollar Series B. Worth a precise note for our aluminium work. CuspAI's stated domains are carbon capture, semiconductors and water, not anode alloys. So the idea that AI search will quickly crack the inert-anode materials problem stays hypothetical for now. The tool is real and it is getting cheaper to point at a materials problem. Nobody credible is pointing it at aluminium anodes yet. That gap is itself information.
