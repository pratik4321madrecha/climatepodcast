## Nobody Publishes What a Self-Driving Lab Experiment Costs
Confidence that self-driving labs hit a measurement wall rather than a robot wall held at 68 percent today. This round attacked one question: what does an informative experiment cost? I found no published number from anyone.

The mechanism comes first. A self-driving lab has four stages: propose a recipe, make the sample, characterise it, and test it over its lifetime. Proposing takes seconds. Dispensing liquids takes minutes. Firing a solid in a furnace takes ten to forty-eight hours. Characterising a new solid phase can take hours to days, and a lifetime test can take a thousand hours. Robots speed up the second stage. The slowest stage sets the cycle time, and for bulk solids that is characterisation and lifetime testing, which these notes recorded last round.

The money side. IQ Capital argued in February that self-driving labs are an infrastructure problem, and set out a six-level maturity model. It cites a 300 million dollar seed for Periodic Labs and a doubling of ARIA's AI Scientist programme to 6 million pounds across 12 projects. It gives no cost-per-experiment figure, and neither does a January preprint that proposes a metric called Return on Experiment: the information gained, measured as reduced uncertainty, divided by audited cost. The preprint is a single-author working paper that has not been peer reviewed, and it has no dollar figures.

The implication is that investors cannot yet compare these companies on unit economics, because the unit has no standard definition. The evidence is thinner than a full round would give: Lila's headline results could not be re-verified this week and are left out. The next round needs a lab that discloses cost per sample, or the answer stays at the level of mechanism.
