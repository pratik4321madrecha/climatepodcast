## The UK Deep-Tech Week: Public Money Into Compute, Out of Carbon Capture
Start with the money, because this fortnight the United Kingdom government moved real amounts in two directions at once. Into deep tech: a fifty million pound, three year ARIA programme called Universal Fabricators, with three Cambridge teams among the eleven, including protein-grown magnets and battery electrodes out of the Institute for Manufacturing. On top of that, roughly fifty million from U K R I for quantum, plus twenty million for quantum networking and eight million for training. And out of climate hardware: the autumn Budget cut carbon-capture capital grants by two hundred million pounds, hitting the Acorn cluster, and low-carbon hydrogen funding by five hundred million, with the money redirected to defence. So the state is betting on compute and fabrication and pulling back from first-of-a-kind carbon plants.

Now the three sectors, Cambridge first. In A I for science and materials, Cambridge produced two tells. Illumion, a university spin-out, raised one-point-seven million for A I imaging of battery materials, backed by ZEISS Ventures and Cambridge Enterprise. And the Innovate Cambridge summit put a number on the ecosystem: Greater Cambridge early-stage companies have raised nine-point-one billion pounds since twenty fifteen, with the average deal growing fivefold to ten million. Up in Liverpool, Matter Machine raised four million for A I materials discovery, led by I P Group.

In quantum, chips and photonics, Nvidia named Riverlane, the Cambridge quantum-error-correction company, among its planned U K investments, which couples the classical A I and quantum roadmaps explicitly. Oxford's Quantum Fabrix won a six-point-eight million Innovate U K contract for a compact strontium optical clock, the kind of timing hardware data centres and satellites need.

In climate and energy, beyond the ARIA award, Cambridge's Echion signed a partnership with a Korean firm to scale its niobium fast-charge anode. The pattern across all three: British science spins out brilliantly, then raises its growth money from American funds and manufactures abroad. Kleiner Perkins into CuspAI, Symbotic into Nyobolt, Nvidia into Riverlane, Echion to Korea. The science is first-rate. The scale-up balance sheet still mostly is not British, and this Budget widened that gap.

Three terms to carry into the week. First, the external laser source. In co-packaged optics, where the light engine sits next to the switch chip, the laser is the part most likely to fail, so the industry is standardising a way to put the laser in a pluggable module on the front panel, the coolest spot, where it can be hot-swapped. It is the fix for co-packaged optics' one real weakness. Second, a foundation interatomic potential. That is a single machine-learning model trained on millions of quantum chemistry calculations that can predict how almost any set of atoms behaves in seconds, instead of running the physics for days. It is the engine under the A I materials discovery startups. Third, first-of-a-kind, often shortened to F O A K. It is the first full-scale commercial plant for a new climate technology, and it is the single hardest thing to finance, because it costs tens or hundreds of millions and falls between venture equity and project finance. That gap is exactly what the Budget's carbon-capture cut just made wider.

## Co-Packaged Optics Ships, But Serviceability Now Decides Who Wins
Confidence on the co-packaged optics thesis moved from sixty-six to seventy percent today. Here is the claim being tested. In the networking that connects the chips inside an A I data centre, co-packaged optics, which means building the light engine right next to the switch chip instead of in a plug-in module, is crossing from demo to real deployment, and the durable profit sits in the laser and photonic integration layer, not in the switch chip and not in cheap assembly.

What moved. At the Open Compute summit this autumn, the analysts at Dell Oro put Nvidia's Spectrum-X Ethernet photonics switch into production, with first customers deploying. That is the crossing the thesis predicted. But their own verdict is the important part. Early commercialisation should not be confused with broad adoption. And the tell is this. Nvidia, the company most associated with co-packaged optics, is also co-proposing a brand new pluggable module standard with Cisco, a twelve-point-eight terabit part. The champion of the new approach is hedging on the old one.

So why does the profit still land where the thesis says? Because the question has shifted from power to serviceability. A faulty plug-in optic is swapped in seconds. A fault inside a co-packaged switch can mean pulling the whole switch. The industry fix is the external laser source, where the laser sits outside and can be replaced without opening the box, and Lumentum's own engineering paper says laser reliability will be one of the deciding factors in which vendor gets chosen. So the laser is both the reliability bottleneck and the selection criterion. That is exactly why Lumentum earns a fifty percent gross margin on lasers and Coherent earns forty percent on integrated optics, while Fabrinet, the pure assembly house, earns twelve percent at record volume. Same A I boom, one quarter of the margin.

The under-priced implication. Back the qualified laser and integration layer, not the switch chip and not the packager. And the United Kingdom's real seat here is in Oxford, in optical switching and optical compute, companies like Salience and Lumai, next to a transceiver market that stays captured by the United States and Taiwan. Cambridge does not own this one.

## AI Power Demand Is Now the Gravity of the Whole Transition
The pattern across the week is one force bending everything else toward it: artificial-intelligence electricity demand. Six moves make the shape clear.

First, the United States took its biggest step on the grid in a decade. The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act moved toward a Senate vote. It would lock in federal transmission planning, force regions to plan together, and let the federal regulator approve interstate power lines if a state stalls for a year. It also makes data centres pay for the grid upgrades they cause. The catch is that it also entrenches gas, which is why it splits the climate side.

Second, the state started underwriting nuclear for A I directly. The Department of Energy offered Constellation up to five billion dollars in loans to uprate eleven reactors, explicitly to feed Google, Amazon and Meta. Clean firm power is now a government-financed story riding hyperscaler demand.

Third, the fuel bottleneck surfaced. A Thiel and Kerry backed startup, General Matter, cleared its first nuclear regulatory review in half the usual time to build uranium enrichment on an old Superfund site. America went from over eighty percent of global enrichment to under one percent, and a ban on Russian fuel imports hits at the end of twenty twenty-seven. The nuclear-for-A I thesis has a supply chokepoint, and reshoring it is the next frontier for capital.

Fourth, the money itself is concentrating. Net Zero Insights put third-quarter climate-tech funding at nineteen-point-six billion dollars, down about eight percent, but across just six hundred and eighty-six deals, the fewest since twenty twenty-one. Firm power alone took six-point-three billion. One bright spot for Pratik's home market: United Kingdom equity rose seventy-four percent to one-and-a-half billion. Fewer, larger, later bets, and the early-stage breadth is thinning.

Fifth, the car is becoming the home battery. Ford's new Fathom is the cheapest American E V that can power a house, with a charger about half the price of the last generation. Distributed storage economics just bent for ordinary buyers.

And the one piece of deep science that fits the arc: a paper in Nature Energy showed a molecular catalyst that captures carbon dioxide and converts it for over a thousand hours, clearing the durability bar that has kept capture a lab curiosity. The long view is simple. For the next one to three years, firm, dispatchable, domestically-secured electrons are where the capital, the policy and even the chemistry are heading, and whether clean ones arrive fast enough depends on whether the grid reform clears.
