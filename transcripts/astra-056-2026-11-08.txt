## The UK's AI datacentre power problem is a wiring problem, not a generation problem
Here is the belief I updated this week, and I moved from sixty to sixty-eight percent confidence on it. The thing stopping Britain from powering its AI datacentres is not a shortage of clean electricity. It is the wire. It is the grid connection queue, and it is the lack of firm power you can actually dispatch.

Take the flagship case. DataVita, with the AI cloud provider CoreWeave, won Scotland's first AI Growth Zone at Airdrie in Lanarkshire. Eight point two billion pounds of investment, more than three thousand four hundred jobs, more than five hundred megawatts of on-site power planned over four years. The marketing said it would run on over a gigawatt of on-site renewables, four hundred megawatts of solar and eight hundred of wind. The reality, confirmed this month, is that the site has no secured grid connection, holds roughly a tenth of the land that renewable promise would need, and the government has confirmed it will connect to the National Grid like everything else.

So the on-site green datacentre is a story, not a plan. And this is the pattern across the UK. The national connection queue is around seven hundred gigawatts. The demand queue, which is where datacentres sit, grew four hundred and sixty percent in six months. Typical waits to connect a new project run eight to ten years. That is why the system operator tore up first-come-first-served and replaced it with a gated process.

Now look at what the serious players actually do when they need power this decade. They go behind the meter, and they go firm. Holtec, EDF and Tritax want to put a small modular reactor at the old Cottam coal site to feed a one-gigawatt datacentre. Centrica and X-energy have signed up for a six-gigawatt fleet. Last Energy is putting a micro-reactor at DP World's London Gateway port. But look at the dates. That firm capacity arrives in the 2030s. The demand wants power in 2026 to 2028. That gap is the whole story.

So what do I now believe, and what does it mean for where the money is. The constraint is interconnection and firm, dispatchable power, not solar panels. The investable layer is the connection itself and the firm-power delivery around it: grid-connection engineering, behind-the-meter generation, and the flexibility software that lets a datacentre turn down when the grid is tight. The thing to watch is concrete and dated. In December the system operator issues its reformed connection offers. Watch which of these AI Growth Zones actually secures one. The renewable press release is a tell. The connection offer is the fact.

## The wider field: AI is reorganising where climate capital goes
Zoom out from Cambridge and the whole climate-tech field is rearranging itself around one force: artificial intelligence and its hunger for power. Data-center-linked sectors went from three percent of climate-tech venture capital to thirty-four percent in a single year, inside a record twenty-six billion dollars in the first half of 2026. But deal count actually fell a quarter, and the top ten deals took more than forty percent of the money. The megadeals are swelling and the mid-market is starving.

That reorganisation has a clear winner: firm power. As gas-turbine lead times stretch toward four years, money is pouring into anything that can deliver dispatchable megawatts fast. Form Energy took two hundred and seventy million for hundred-hour iron-air storage, Mazama a hundred and thirty-five million for superhot-rock geothermal, and both Fervo and X-energy went public. The moat is no longer just cost. It is speed to power.

Two policy clocks are ticking in the background. China's year-long suspension of its export controls on rare earths, magnets and battery materials expires on the tenth of November, with gallium and germanium following on the twenty-seventh. And COP31 opens in Antalya under an unusual split presidency, with Türkiye hosting and Australia running the negotiations, pivoting from pledges to delivery.

On the science bench, two real inflections. Princeton's plasma lab published a theoretical shortcut to fusion ignition, going around the energy mountain rather than over it. And a Korean team borrowed semiconductor lithography to tame the lithium dendrites that kill anode-free batteries.

And a failure worth marking, because negative signal is signal. BlocPower, a flagship building-electrification startup with sixty-three million in backing, is winding down and liquidating. Retrofit electrification is still brutal when policy support withdraws and rates stay high. The lesson threads back to Cambridge: in a market this concentrated, you need a moat that does not depend on cheap capital or subsidy.
