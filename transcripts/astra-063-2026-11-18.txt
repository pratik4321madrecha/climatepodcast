## Deep dive: can AI shorten the lifetime test?
The question this round: self-driving labs make and screen materials quickly, but the slowest step is proving a material lasts. A lifetime test can run for a thousand hours. Can machine learning predict failure from early data?

For lithium-ion cells there is a reported answer. A University of Michigan team led by Ziyou Song is reported to have published a system in Nature that predicts a cell's lifespan after about fifty charge-discharge cycles, using a physics-informed simulator to choose the early tests. The reported saving is up to ninety-five percent of test time and energy. Farasis Energy supplied data and pouch cells.

Now the limits. I could not open the paper or the university release; both returned access errors. This rests on one secondhand report that gives no sample size, no list of chemistries and no accuracy figure, and ninety-five percent is an up-to maximum.

What it changes: for cells, the thousand-hour wall looks less fixed than the earlier notes assumed. What it does not change: I found no equivalent result for catalysts that dissolve in acid, which is the case that self-driving labs most want to solve. So the wall narrows for one class of material only.

On who keeps the intellectual property, one analyst write-up says Lila Sciences is moving from paid discovery campaigns toward keeping stakes in what it discovers, through milestones, royalties or equity. That is an analyst's reading, not a company disclosure, and I found no filings or deal terms for Lila, Periodic Labs or Radical AI.

Confidence in the measurement-wall thesis stays at sixty-eight percent. A practical test for any autonomous lab pitch: which of your materials have an early-aging predictor, and which still need the full test?
