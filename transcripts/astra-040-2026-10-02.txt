## The Cambridge Week: One Four-Hundred-Fifty-Million Round Bends the Whole Cluster
The UK deep-tech week came down to one number. CuspAI, a Cambridge company that uses AI to design new materials, closed a four-hundred-fifty-million-dollar round at a two-point-six-billion valuation and launched what it calls an AI Materials Foundry, pooling compute and lab access across more than forty-five organisations. Nothing else in Cambridge this quarter came close. That gap tells you where the gravity is: AI for science is the demand engine, and it pulls directly on the other two sectors Pratik is tracking.

Start with quantum and chips, because the week was busy. Quantinuum ran its new Helix error-correction architecture on its Helios processor and reported a record logical memory error rate, a few parts in a hundred thousand. Riverlane, the Cambridge error-correction specialist, opened a United States headquarters in Maryland and signed a cooperation agreement with Infleqtion inside the UK quantum programme. In London and Oxford, Phasecraft opened a US office and, with Nvidia, reported a fifteen-times speed-up in quantum simulation for drug discovery. Quantum Motion took a second close on its Series C to scale silicon qubits on standard chip lines. The pattern: UK quantum is strong on the hardest layer, error correction and control, and is now planting flags in American defence and compute budgets.

Now climate and energy, where the money is smaller but real. Ki Thirteen, a Carbon Thirteen spinout in Cambridge, raised five million dollars to turn biomass into green hydrogen and biogenic carbon dioxide, the two feedstocks synthetic aviation fuel actually needs. In London, Clean Growth Fund took an eighty-one-million-pound second close of its second fund. And nationally, the UK put twenty-eight million pounds behind very long duration storage as a grant, because, as today's deep dive shows, the thing it will not yet finance with a revenue floor is exactly that frontier.

The one-paragraph map: these three sectors are becoming one story told through compute and atoms. AI-for-materials needs the power electronics and photonics that Cambridge GaN Devices and the Oxford photonics firms build to make data centres efficient, and it points straight at the climate problem the Carbon Thirteen founders are trying to commercialise. Capital concentrates in a Cambridge, Oxford and London triangle, with national money from ARIA and Innovate UK and American strategic money stacked on top. Where the UK is genuinely strong: foundational quantum, graphene and gallium-nitride chips, and the depth of its materials science. Where it is merely loud: late-stage growth capital and manufacturing still lean on foreign cheques.

Three terms to carry into the room this week. Inverse design: instead of starting from a material and predicting its properties, you specify the properties you want and let a generative model propose the structure. A MegaQuOp: a quantum machine able to run one million reliable operations, the field's new yardstick for useful, replacing raw qubit counts. And grid-forming inverters: power electronics that actively set grid voltage and frequency rather than following the grid, the unlock for a renewables-heavy grid that has lost its spinning-machine inertia.

## Where UK Storage Money Actually Banks: The Floor Stops at Twenty-Two Hours
The question this session tested: where does the UK's long-duration storage money actually get banked, and did anything change in the last month? The answer held firm, and hardened. Storage bankability in twenty-twenty-six is gated by a revenue floor, not by the materials cost curve, and the floor has a duration ceiling of about twenty-two hours. Our confidence in the strong form stays around twenty percent; the refined belief is now confirmed and harder than before.

Here is the mechanism. A lender does not underwrite a cheaper battery chemistry. It underwrites a contract: a multi-year revenue floor, either a sovereign one, the UK's cap-and-floor scheme, or a private one, a ten-to-fifteen-year toll. Ofgem's cap-and-floor Window One is now minded to award sixteen projects, seven-point-six gigawatts, one hundred thirty-seven gigawatt-hours, out of seventy-three that applied. But not one has reached financial close yet; final awards come this autumn. And everything in that window sits between eight and twenty-two hours: lithium, some vanadium flow, pumped hydro.

Genuine multiday storage, the hundred-hour iron-air batteries from Form Energy, got no floor at all. Instead the government routed it to a twenty-eight-million-pound research grant. That is the whole thesis in one contrast: the floor banks long duration, but not multiday. Form Energy itself proves the point from the other side. It is scaling on seven hundred fifty million dollars of fresh equity at a lower valuation, a two hundred seventy-million-dollar credit facility led by Barclays, a United States Department of Energy loan to its rival Eos, and a one-billion-dollar Google offtake, every route except a merchant revenue floor. And the one flow-battery maker without a floor or an offtake, ESS Tech, ended the quarter with three-and-a-half million dollars in cash and a going-concern warning.

The single number that would settle the cheap-iron story still does not exist. After months of commercial operation, no hundred-hour player has disclosed an audited delivered cost per kilowatt-hour or a measured field round-trip efficiency. The public figures are targets. That silence is itself the finding.

So the contrarian read: the cheapest-capex chemistry loses to the one a counterparty will write a floor against. The durable rent has migrated past the electrode to the revenue contract. For a UK investor or founder, the tradeable seat is not the battery. It is floor structuring, revenue-contract origination, and duration and flexibility optimisation. Watch two triggers: the first cap-and-floor project to reach financial close, and the first hundred-hour player to publish a real cost-and-efficiency number. Until then, iron-air is a twenty-twenty-eight bet, not a twenty-twenty-six one.

## The Grid and the Contract Are Now the Binding Constraint
The wider field this fortnight kept pointing at one shift: the binding constraint on the energy transition has moved from the cost of generation to the grid and the contract. Four arcs show it.

First, the grid said no to the data centre. The governor of Texas ordered the state environment agency to halt all new data-centre permits until the grid operator finishes a reliability and water audit. The bottleneck for AI compute is now interconnection and local political licence, not chips.

Second, flexible load became the new peaker. Emerald AI raised one hundred fifty million dollars at a one-billion valuation after showing a cluster of graphics chips cut its power draw by a quarter for three hours. Demand flexibility is now being equity-financed as a substitute for building firm capacity.

Third, climate capital is concentrating into fewer, bigger bets. Form Energy's seven-hundred-fifty-million-dollar round came at a lower valuation even as its backlog quadrupled, the signature of a market crowding into perceived winners while early-stage funding thins.

Fourth, the deep-tech science kept moving. A team reported the first all-perovskite triple-junction solar cell above thirty percent efficiency, with independent certification at twenty-nine-point-three. Peak efficiency is closing on silicon's practical ceiling; the open question is whether it survives at module scale outdoors.

The long view: solar and storage are cheap. What decides what actually gets built now is the queue, the permit, and who underwrites the revenue. AI demand is the swing buyer twice over, the load that strains the grid and the balance sheet that finances firm capacity to feed it.
