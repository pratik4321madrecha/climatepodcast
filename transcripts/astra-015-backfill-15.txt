What the coating physically does, and why the physics was never the question
Passive daytime radiative cooling is an engineered surface that cools itself below the surrounding air using zero electricity, and it works by stacking two optical tricks on one coating. First it reflects roughly 95-98% of incoming sunlight, so it barely absorbs any solar heat. Second it emits its own thermal energy as infrared light precisely in the 8-13 micron "atmospheric window," the narrow band where the sky is transparent, giving the surface a direct radiative path to the roughly -270°C of deep space. The surface therefore sheds more heat to the sky than it takes in and drops below ambient air even in direct sun. The nearest familiar picture is a greenhouse run in reverse: colour and heat governed by structure, not by power.
On the merits, the device is real, replicated and cheap, which is exactly why physics is a settled input rather than the open question. The effect has been demonstrated repeatedly since the Stanford result in 2014 (CU Boulder 2017, Columbia 2018, Purdue's barium-sulphate paint in 2021), and the raw material runs about $0.39-1.20/m2. The single field-measured deployment, the California Energy Commission's CEC-500-2025-002 refrigeration demo - https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/CEC-500-2025-002.pdf, confirms the surface does what the papers say. So we went in asking a product question, not a physics one: has this become a standalone financial upgrade you buy in 2026 on cost alone?

The gating layer: five facts that decide who captures the value
The settling number, an audited unsubsidised installed-cost payback, does not exist anywhere. The one field-measured demonstration, the CEC's state-subsidised refrigeration trial - https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/CEC-500-2025-002.pdf, reports a 15% saving and 27,000 kWh but discloses neither installed cost nor payback. The opacity survived a government-funded demo. Every attractive figure quoted for supermarket CO2 subcooling, the 1.6-4.9 year paybacks, is modelled, not banked.
The category leader cannot raise, which is the market's own verdict on the payback. SkyCool Systems' Series A has been open 14+ months - https://www.propelx.com/blog/skycool-systems-radiative-cooling-climate-tech/: about $9.4M raised, the round set in September 2025, and still "targeting" $10-15M as of May 2026. The revenue is genuine, $3.33M at 10x year-on-year, but grant-heavy. A company sitting on a two-year payback does not run on grants and hide the number.
When the market's judges picked the best data-centre-cooling idea of 2026, they picked the bundle, not the bolt-on. SkyCool was only a finalist. The reward went to efficiency that disappears into incumbent infrastructure over a surface that bolts on top of it.

Ecosystem Placement
Radiative cooling is a demand-reduction layer of the energy transition, and in 2026 that layer only pays when it slots into an infrastructure someone is already financing. Cooling is one of the fastest-growing electricity loads on the planet, and a surface that sheds heat with no power should be a gift to it. The reason it is not a business is structural, not thermal: the value of a passive augment accrues to whoever owns the loop, the roof or the refrigeration contract it improves. It sits alongside dynamic line rating and heat-transfer fluids as an efficiency wedge, useful, real, and almost always captured by the incumbent rather than the inventor. The transition needs the physics; the returns go to integration.

The question was one number: a real, audited, unsubsidised payback with the installed cost disclosed. It doesn't exist anywhere. The one field demo (state-funded) reports the saving and omits the cost. The category leader's Series A has been open 14 months on grants. Opacity is the verdict.
