The spine · thesis (the honest fit of a low-but-stable magnet)
Iron-nitride is a genuinely good magnet in the wrong argument: it wins on stored energy and loses on the one property a car motor is built around. The material is Fe16N2 (alpha-double-prime iron nitride, eight iron atoms arranged around interstitial nitrogen), and it has a saturation magnetisation near 2.9 tesla, higher than neodymium's, so a given lump can in principle store a lot of magnetic push. But a permanent magnet is graded on two numbers, not one. Energy product (BHmax, the magnetic energy stored per unit volume, in mega-gauss-oersteds, MGOe) sets how much torque the lump delivers; coercivity (the reverse magnetic field it takes to demagnetise it, in kilo-oersteds, kOe) sets whether it keeps that torque when something pushes back. Iron-nitride's problem is the second number: its magnetocrystalline anisotropy, the internal "grain" that pins the magnetisation in one direction, is weak, so a 2025 Wiley review frames the material for uses working below 150C and not requesting high coercivity - https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/metm.70022.
Coercivity-at-temperature is the hard axis because a rotor is hot and a hot magnet forgets, and this is where the physics ceiling is now quantified, not asserted. A traction motor rotor runs at 150-180C, and heat erodes the field a magnet holds; the incumbent, sintered NdFeB doped with the heavy rare earths dysprosium and terbium, exists precisely because Dy and Tb prop up coercivity at those temperatures. Think of coercivity as the grip that stops a hot magnet from quietly forgetting it was ever magnetised. The 2025 primary literature pins the gap: iron-nitride's anisotropy field is about 1.1 tesla versus Nd2Fe14B's 7.3, and the best dopant (tungsten) still leaves it roughly three times short - https://arxiv.org/html/2508.01035; the best magnet actually manufactured in bulk reached coercivity near 1.09 kOe and decomposes above about 250C - https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4352/15/11/969, and the best thin film only 884 oersted - https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/15/3/035319/3339488/Unusually-high-coercivity-of-sputtered-Fe16N2-thin. A traction rotor needs 10-30 kOe. That is not a scale-up gap that funding closes; it is a property of the crystal.
The one genuine physics advantage is the whole commercial story: iron-nitride is low-but-stable, not high-but-fading, and that is a ferrite fit, not a rotor fit. The new nuance this session is the temperature coefficient of coercivity, how fast the grip fades as the magnet heats: iron-nitride loses almost nothing, about +0.4 oersted per kelvin over 27-152C, two orders of magnitude flatter than NdFeB's -81.9 - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S135964541930792X. So it holds its modest coercivity at a hot rotor without any Dy or Tb at all. That is exactly the profile of hard ferrite, the cheap grey magnet in a loudspeaker or a fan, only with more stored energy and no rare earth. The numbers make the segment concrete.

Ecosystem placement
Magnets are the physical bottleneck under the entire electrification, robotics and AI-data-centre build, so this is a chokepoint story, not a chemistry one. The rent goes to whoever controls processing, recycling and Western manufacturing, not the mine and not the headline chemistry. Every electric drivetrain, every humanoid actuator, every liquid-cooling pump for a GPU rack pulls on the same NdFeB supply chain, gated at the heavy-rare-earth refining step that sits almost entirely in China. Iron-nitride is one hedge against that chokepoint; recycling and de-Dy/Tb NdFeB are the other two, and today they are the ones with binding contracts behind them. The magnet is where the AI and defence build meets the mineral map, which is why it prices on supply security now, not on climate, and why the low-but-stable niche can be a real business while the traction bet stays a hedge.
Both camps are wrong. Iron-nitride's low-but-stable coercivity makes it a genuinely good ferrite-replacement that needs no rare earth at all, and at the same time a physical non-starter for a traction rotor, and the money in the "rare-earth crisis" is not in new chemistry but in recycling and de-Dy/Tb NdFeB. The tell is the contract structure: GM, ABB, Bosch and the Pentagon signed binding offtake for China-free and recycled NdFeB, while the challenger chemistry raised strategic equity. When buyers pay take-or-pay for the incumbent and options premium for the challenger, the challenger is a hedge, not a default, and its own first product, a loudspeaker magnet, agrees.
